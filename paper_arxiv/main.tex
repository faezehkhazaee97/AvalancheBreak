\begin{abstract}
Inserting a content-free marker (\texttt{|}) into the input every $k$ characters of a character-level transformation, and asking the model to copy it through, is a cheap single-call intervention. We study what it does across seven tasks, seven open instruction-tuned models, and two versions of Gemini Flash (3.5 and 3.6), using two controls that fix tokenization at one token per character: the same letter tasks with spaced letters, and exact relabelings with digits. We find three separate effects. First, on letter strings, markers act largely as re-tokenization: at five of the seven open models and at Gemini 3.6, spacing the letters with no markers improves accuracy more ($+0.16$ to $+0.33$, and to $+0.30$ among the open models alone) than markers do ($+0.13$ to $+0.26$). Second, the gain survives one token per character: with spaced letters or digits, markers remove 33\% to 83\% of the remaining errors at the four strongest open models, and the gains persist to $N = 256$ at Qwen2.5-32B and are matched there by commas or line breaks as markers. Third, with a marker every two or more characters, the position right after a marker fails whenever its output depends on the input before it: on four dependency tasks it falls to near chance at the stronger models while the next position recovers, and rewriting the tasks so that position needs nothing before the marker repairs it. Blocking marker-attending heads removes part of the gain, and a constrained-decoding ablation on four tasks at Qwen2.5-32B shows that marking either input or output alone hurts while marking both helps, and that on \task{running\_xor} the failure at the crossing position is not specific to the input side. Real promoter DNA and English text show the same gain and failure. In practice: separate characters before adding markers, mark long sequences sparsely on input and output, and keep markers away from positions whose output depends on what came just before them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask a language model to apply a simple, fully specified rule to every character of a string (shift each letter forward, substitute through a lookup table, output the XOR of each digit with the one before) and accuracy does not fade gracefully with length. It holds near ceiling on short inputs and then collapses well before any obvious capacity limit \citep{hou2025focus}. Standard remedies change the computation itself: splitting the input across several calls \citep{zhou2023leasttomost}, asking for intermediate steps \citep{nye2021scratchpad, chen2023pot}, or adding memory to the architecture \citep{dai2019transformerxl, bulatov2022rmt, mohtashami2023landmark}. Each costs extra calls, extra tokens, or training.

We study a cheaper intervention. A content-free marker character (\texttt{|}) is inserted into the input every $k$ characters, and the model is told to copy it through to the output unchanged. The marker carries no task information, needs no extra call, and requires no training. On letter tasks it raises accuracy substantially, by up to $+0.26$ on average at the stronger models we test. Our question is why, and when it hurts.

A marker does more than mark a boundary. Tokenizers merge runs of letters into multi-letter tokens, and a marker after every few letters splits those runs, so on letter strings a marker is also a tokenization intervention. We separate the two with two controls that keep one token per character whether or not markers are present: the same letter tasks with a space between letters, and exact relabelings of them with digits. We find three effects (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{On letter strings, markers largely re-tokenize} (Section~\ref{sec:tokenization}). At all five Qwen and Gemma models and at Gemini 3.6, spacing the letters with no markers at all helps more than markers on unspaced letters ($+0.16$ to $+0.33$ against $+0.13$ to $+0.26$); at Mistral and Phi the two help about equally. The striking accuracy of the position right after a marker on letter tasks (positive in all 27 task--model cells, with per-model means of $+0.07$ to $+0.34$ over the no-marker run at the same positions) is largely a token-boundary effect: with spaced letters or digits that position is no better than the rest of its segment.
\item \textbf{The gain persists when each character is its own token} (Section~\ref{sec:segmentation}). With spaced letters or digits, markers remove 33\% to 83\% of the remaining errors at Qwen2.5-14B and 32B, Qwen3.8-27B, and Gemma-4-31B, and the two controls agree closely. The gain is absent at short lengths, where these models are near ceiling, appears at $N = 32$, and at $N = 64$ reaches $+0.26$ (spaced letters) and $+0.22$ (digits), with little dependence on spacing. At Qwen2.5-32B it persists to $N = 256$ (56\% of remaining errors removed) and is the same with commas or line breaks as markers. At the three smallest models the gains are smaller and inconsistent (11 of these 18 cells significantly positive and one significantly negative).
\item \textbf{The position after a marker fails on dependencies that cross it} (Section~\ref{sec:blindspot}). With a marker every two or more characters, on tasks where each output also depends on the previous input, the position right after a marker falls below the no-marker run in 33 of 35 task--model cells (four tasks across nine models, except \task{sliding\_window\_max} at Gemini 3.5, which was not run there; every cell at the six strongest models), to near chance at the stronger models, even where the model is otherwise near ceiling. The next position recovers immediately, and rewriting the tasks so that this position needs nothing before the marker repairs it. The loss is not created by how the instruction describes the markers: telling the model they are only visual separators and that the rule continues across them narrows it, and removes it only at one model on two of the four tasks, and commas or line breaks in place of \texttt{|} leave the position at chance up to $N = 256$. Pooled task-level effects on these tasks are consistent with a mix of this loss and the within-segment gains of Finding~2, which is why their sign varies with length and model.
\end{enumerate}

We also test the obvious mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). In Qwen2.5, some heads attend from each position to the adjacent marker, but at Qwen2.5-32B and $N = 64$ blocking the ten most marker-attending heads removes 1\% to 19\% of the gain, against at most 5\% for controls, and blocking 200 removes 21\% to 28\%, against none for controls of the same size: marker-directed attention contributes, but it is not concentrated in the heads that attend to markers most. A $2 \times 2$ separating input from output markers under constrained decoding, on four tasks at Qwen2.5-32B (Section~\ref{sec:sides}), finds that marking either side alone \emph{hurts} ($-0.03$ to $-0.16$) while marking both helps ($+0.02$ to $+0.39$), and that the failure at the crossing position is not specific to the input side. Section~\ref{sec:practical} turns the findings into guidance.

\paragraph{Supporting analyses.} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:summary} lists the checks behind each finding, from alignment-robust scoring and other delimiters to activation patching and a finetuned separator, and Section~\ref{sec:real} repeats the design on real DNA and English text; none changes the sign of any of the three effects. Two are worth naming here because they bound what the effects depend on. The models are instruction-tuned, but the effects are not an artifact of instruction tuning: the base checkpoints of Qwen2.5-7B and 32B show the blind spot at close to its instruct size (Appendix~\ref{app:base}). And the blind spot is not simply a misunderstanding of the instruction: telling the model in words that the position after a bar uses the input before it, with two worked examples of that step, removes most of the loss at Gemma 4, about half at Qwen3.8, and about a third at Qwen2.5-32B, but not all of it at any model (Section~\ref{sec:repair}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Tokenization, formatting, and position.} Subword tokenizers obscure the characters inside a token: models know only partially which characters their tokens contain \citep{kaushal2022tokens}, character-level manipulation is a known weakness \citep{edman2024cute}, and longer tokens obscure where a character sits \citep{uzan2026charbench}. Models handle non-canonical encodings of the same string surprisingly well, splitting text into characters can improve string manipulation \citep{zheng2025broken}, retokenizing a prompt while preserving its bytes probes segmentation sensitivity \citep{jain2026retokenization}, and instruction-tuned models handle non-canonical tokenizations robustly after instruction tuning \citep{zheng2025broken}, which is one reason our spaced-letter control works at all; our synthetic strings are uniform random letters with no words in them, so the lexical word recovery of \citet{yang2026wordrecovery} bears on the English text of Section~\ref{sec:real} rather than on those. Surface formatting can change few-shot accuracy by tens of points \citep{sclar2024quantifying}, token costs vary across languages and formats \citep{petrov2023unfairness, ahia2023cost}, and the surface form of numbers \citep{nogueira2021arithmetic}, positional description \citep{shen2023positional}, and the presentation of positions \citep{duan2023interpolation} change arithmetic accuracy and length generalization. Closest to the markers we study is \emph{index hinting} \citep{zhou2024algorithms}, which interleaves labels drawn from an ordered set into both the query and the response, so that \texttt{42+39=81} is presented as \texttt{a4b2+a3b9=a8b1} and corresponding positions can be matched by the identity of their label; it is needed for length generalization on addition, and removing it makes every position encoding fail \citep{zhou2024robustly}. Our markers carry the opposite property. One symbol repeats every $k$ characters and labels nothing, so a position can only be located by counting from the last boundary. That is enough to hold alignment inside a segment, and it is also what leaves the step across a boundary with nothing to address, which is where the blind spot appears. Two things differ between index hinting and our markers, and they should not be run together: index hinting gives \emph{every} character its own label, so both operands of a one-step rule carry consecutive keys, whereas our markers label only the segment boundaries. Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} tests the boundary-labelling half directly, with distinct ordered labels at $k = 4$, and they do not remove the blind spot; that rules out confusion between one boundary and another, not per-character addressing, which we do not test. These works change how text is encoded. We hold the encoding fixed instead: our controls give one token per character whether or not markers are present, so a marker's remaining effect cannot be a change in how characters are grouped into tokens, and the post-marker failure can be attributed to the boundary rather than to the tokenizer.

\paragraph{Learned segmentation.} Tokenizer-free models learn where to place boundaries: H-Net learns content-dependent chunks over bytes end to end \citep{hwang2025hnet}, H-Net++ adds a cross-chunk attention module because chunking alone ignored dependencies across chunks \citep{zakershahrak2025hnetpp}, MrT5 learns to delete byte tokens partway through the encoder \citep{kallini2025mrt5}, Charformer learns soft subword blocks from characters \citep{tay2022charformer}, and SOMBRERO steers learned boundaries toward hard-to-predict positions \citep{neitemeier2026sombrero}. Byte-level models still segment the byte stream: ByT5 reads raw bytes \citep{xue2022byt5}, MegaByte groups them into fixed patches \citep{yu2023megabyte}, and the Byte Latent Transformer into entropy-based patches \citep{pagnoni2024blt}. Our markers are the opposite case, fixed content-free boundaries inserted at inference time into a model never trained to use them. Whether the first position after a learned or fixed patch boundary also loses access to what came before it is open; the H-Net++ design suggests that dependencies across boundaries need explicit support there as well.

\paragraph{Attention sinks and separator tokens.} Language models concentrate attention on a few tokens, most famously the first, which act as sinks \citep{xiao2024streaming, gu2024sink, zhang2025catch}; \citet{su2026sinksurvey} survey uses and interpretations. SepLLM shows that separator tokens can summarize the segment before them \citep{chen2024sepllm}, SinkLoRA exploits sinks during fine-tuning \citep{zhang2024sinklora}, punctuation tokens have served as anchors for trained sparse attention \citep{qiu2026phsa}, and tabular structure in a prompt improves factual data interactions \citep{oh2024tables}. Closest to our intervention, SepSeq \citep{sun2026sepseq} inserts a separator every $k$ items into long numerical inputs at inference time, without training, to counter the dispersion of softmax attention, and improves sequence-level answers such as counts and maxima; there only the input is segmented and the answer is a single value. Our tasks are per-position transformations whose output is written segment by segment, and there we separate re-tokenization from segmentation, find that segmenting the input alone hurts unless the output is segmented too (Section~\ref{sec:sides}), and find that the position after a separator loses the dependency across it. SepLLM's and SinkLoRA's separators are learned or exploited in training. Ours are inserted at inference time into models never trained for this use, so any effect must come from the boundary rather than from task content, although the bar may carry structural associations from pretraining, which is one reason we also test commas and line breaks. \citet{dangal2026anchoring} find that inserting a comma at a clause boundary reduces degradation of later predictions while biasing attention toward existing punctuation does not, because the inserted token brings a new hidden state computed from the full context; our marker is such a token, and the weak effect of blocking attention to it fits that reading.

\paragraph{Attention heads.} Induction heads implement in-context copying \citep{olsson2022induction}, and causal head gating assigns roles to heads by intervention \citep{nam2025headgating}. We characterize the marker-attending heads and block them from the markers. On character-counting probes, character-level information is encoded in early layers and then actively suppressed by late-layer components, with no attention heads specializing in routing it \citep{datta2026suppression}; our finding that the marker benefit is not concentrated in the heads that attend to markers most is consistent with that picture.

\paragraph{Long sequences, decomposition, and benchmarks.} Models use the middle of long contexts poorly \citep{liu2024lost}, make sparse but systematic errors on state tracking \citep{liu2023flipflop}, fail long-horizon tasks for reasons that compound \citep{wang2026mirage, pushkin2026lead, anand2026periodic}, and show an accuracy cliff on repetitive deterministic tasks of the kind we use \citep{hou2025focus}. Remedies range from architecture, such as SWAN's interleaved global and sliding-window layers \citep{puvvada2025swan}, ALiBi \citep{press2022alibi} and FIRE \citep{li2024fire}, Transformer-XL \citep{dai2019transformerxl}, recurrent memory \citep{bulatov2022rmt}, landmark tokens \citep{mohtashami2023landmark}, and learned context management \citep{kontonis2026memento}, through inference-time reshaping of attention or rotary frequencies \citep{han2024lminfinite, wang2024resonance}, to prompting that decomposes the computation \citep{zhou2023leasttomost, chen2023pot, nye2021scratchpad}; content-free markers sit at the cheap end of this spectrum, and the tokenization-independent benefit we find is a long-sequence effect of this kind. Boundaries matter for probabilistic conditioning as well: byte-level sampling methods exist because a prompt that ends mid-token distorts the next-token distribution \citep{hayase2025bytesampler}, and the failure we document is a deterministic, per-position counterpart at an inserted boundary. Our tasks are generated fresh for every trial, following BeyondBench \citep{srivastava2025beyondbench}; external checks use ProcBench \citep{fujisawa2024procbench}, the copying task of \citet{jelassi2024repeat}, and CLRS-Text \citep{markeeva2024clrstext}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

This section describes the tasks and the three formats they are posed in, the marker intervention, the models and their decoding settings, and the metrics and statistics used throughout.

\subsection{Tasks and Formats}
\label{sec:tasks}

Every task maps an input string of length $N$ to an output string of the same length under a deterministic rule stated fully in the prompt (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). In \emph{position-local} tasks, output $y_i$ depends only on input $x_i$. In \emph{one-step dependency} tasks, $y_i$ also depends on $x_{i-1}$; in the \emph{windowed} task, on $x_{i-2}, x_{i-1}, x_i$. For dependency tasks, the first position is copied as-is ($y_0 = x_0$), and \task{sliding\_window\_max} takes the available prefix at the second, $y_1 = \max(x_0, x_1)$. Inputs are sampled uniformly with a deterministic seed per (task, length, spacing, alphabet size, trial), so every model sees the same inputs; because the seed includes the spacing, different spacings, including the no-marker run, draw independent inputs from the same distribution, so main-grid comparisons are unpaired (a paired-input rerun agrees to within 0.01; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:paired}). \task{copy\_previous} requires no computation, only a one-step lookback, so any harm from markers cannot be blamed on arithmetic; \task{sliding\_window\_max} uses a three-symbol window, so its output stays informative at every length.

The position-local tasks are posed in three formats. In the \emph{letter} format (the default), letters are written contiguously, and tokenizers merge them: at each of the seven open models, whose tokenizers we can inspect, 48 random letters encode to 23 to 32 tokens without markers and, with a marker after each letter, to one token per letter plus one per marker, 95 tokens in all. In the \emph{spaced} format, the same letters are separated by single spaces and the model is asked to answer in the same format; the 48 letters encode to exactly 48 tokens at every open model whether or not markers are present, and each marker adds one token of its own. In the \emph{digit} format, the letters are relabeled as digits ($A \to 0$, $B \to 1$, \dots), which every open-model tokenizer we test encodes one per character. On Gemini 3.6 Flash, whose tokenizer we cannot inspect, the API's token counts show exactly one token per added character and one per marker in both controls, against 0.46 tokens per contiguous letter (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:geminitokens}). Both controls are exact transformations of the letter tasks: same seeds, same positions, and the same rule, verified in every cell. The wording is not quite uniform, and we do not claim it is: the digit prompt for \task{cyclic\_shift} is reworded for digits, while those for \task{random\_permutation} and \task{conditional\_shift} relabel the symbols but keep the word ``character'' in the task line and rule (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The comparison each control supports is therefore within a task, where the wording is held fixed, not across them. The dependency tasks use digits throughout. One caveat on \task{conditional\_shift}: its rule depends on a symbol's position in the alphabet, which for digits equals the digit itself, so its digit version is easier.

\subsection{The Marker Intervention}

In the marker condition (``Bounds''), a \texttt{|} is inserted after every $k$ input characters, with $k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$; only $k < N$ is used. The expected output is the ground truth computed on the continuous, marker-free sequence, with markers spliced in at the same positions. The prompt states the rule, then instructs, for example: \emph{``The input contains vertical bars (|). These are structural boundaries. DO NOT transform them. Copy them exactly to the output.''} (the verb matches the task, e.g.\ ``shift'' for \task{cyclic\_shift}). The no-marker baseline (``Base'') uses the identical prompt, including that instruction, so that the only difference between conditions is whether markers appear in the input. That instruction is odd for an input with no bars in it, so we measured what it costs: rerunning the no-marker condition with every mention of markers removed, on the same inputs, across 300 (task, length, alphabet) cells at the four stronger models, shifts accuracy by less than a percentage point: $+0.006$ on average (95\% interval $[0.002, 0.009]$), at most $+0.026$ in any model--family combination, and $-0.003 \pm 0.009$ at Qwen2.5-7B. Appendix~\ref{app:stats} gives the breakdown. The marker effects we report are therefore overstated by well under a percentage point on average against a baseline whose prompt never mentions bars. Example prompts are in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. Runs also included $k = 3$, which we exclude from the pooled power-of-two aggregates but analyze separately, with $k = 5$ and 6, as a check on periodicity (Section~\ref{sec:blindspot}, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blindk}).

\subsection{Models and Decoding}

Open models are Qwen2.5-7B/14B/32B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024qwen25}, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 \citep{jiang2023mistral}, Phi-3.5-mini-instruct \citep{abdin2024phi3}, Qwen3.8-27B \citep{qwen2026qwen38}, and Gemma-4-31B-it \citep{gemma2026gemma4}, run locally in bfloat16 with each model's chat template and no system message beyond the template's own default (thinking disabled for Qwen3.8 and Gemma 4) and greedy decoding. Runs use PyTorch 2.6.0 (CUDA 12.4) and Transformers 5.12.1 with the eager attention implementation, on NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs (Titan RTX for some smaller-model runs); Appendix~\ref{app:trials} lists the exact checkpoint revisions. Qwen3.8-27B is a hybrid architecture: 48 of its 64 layers use Gated DeltaNet linear attention \citep{yang2025gated} and only 16 use softmax attention; Gemma-4-31B-it uses softmax attention in every layer (50 sliding-window and 10 global layers). The generation budget is $1.5N + 20$ new tokens, or $2N + 20$ at $k = 1$, where a correct marked answer is $2N - 1$ tokens if the model emits separate space and character tokens rather than the merged space-prefixed ones of Section~\ref{sec:tasks}; predictions are clipped to the answer's length before scoring, and among the 86,400 stored raw generations a budget cut-off affects at most 0.6\% of any condition class and 0.04\% at the four stronger models, nearly all of it Phi-3.5-mini on spaced letters (Appendix~\ref{app:trials}, which also lists which batches store raw output). The closed models are two versions of Gemini Flash, queried through the API with the same prompts and seeds and with thinking disabled: Gemini 3.5 Flash \citep{gemini35flash} at temperature 0 on the letter and dependency tasks with 3 trials per cell, and, after Google began routing its requests to a newer model \citep{geminichangelog}, Gemini 3.6 Flash \citep{gemini36flash} on all 13 task--format variants with 5 trials per cell. Gemini 3.6 ignores the temperature parameter, so its outputs are samples rather than greedy decodes; with 5 trials per cell its per-cell estimates carry wider sampling variability than the open models' 20 to 80 generations, and we read them as directional confirmation across conditions rather than as precise per-cell effects. Appendix~\ref{app:trials} gives the query dates and the checks on the served model and its usage metadata. Qwen2.5-32B is our primary model for head-level experiments.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}

\emph{Character accuracy} is the fraction of output positions whose character matches the ground truth, after removing every marker and space from both the prediction and the ground truth, trimming outer whitespace, and clipping the cleaned prediction to the ground-truth length: for an answer $y_1 \dots y_N$ and a cleaned prediction $\hat y$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{acc} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\left[\hat y_i = y_i\right],
\label{eq:acc}
\end{equation}
with a missing $\hat y_i$ counted as an error. We avoid the name ``token accuracy'' deliberately: the unit is a character of the answer, not a tokenizer token. Marker placement is not part of this metric; Appendix~\ref{app:wfr} reports exact transformation match and marker-placement compliance separately.

\paragraph{Pooled effects.} Only spacings $k < N$ are run, so marker runs are concentrated at longer lengths than no-marker runs, and accuracy falls with length. A pooled effect therefore compares the mean character accuracy of the marker runs with the no-marker mean reweighted to the same length distribution,
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \overline{\mathrm{acc}}_{\text{Bounds}} - \sum_N p_N\, \overline{\mathrm{acc}}_{\text{Base},N},
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
where $p_N$ is the share of marker runs at length $N$; it pools $N \le 64$, $k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$, and alphabet or modulus values, and $z$ is $\Delta$ over its standard error (\Eqref{eq:se}). Because the controls leave strong models near ceiling, we also report the \emph{share of remaining errors removed},
\begin{equation}
\rho = \frac{\bar\Delta}{1 - \overline{\mathrm{acc}}_{\text{Base}}},
\label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
where $\bar\Delta$ is the unweighted mean of $\Delta$ over a model's tasks and the denominator is one minus the mean length-matched no-marker accuracy. Because that denominator vanishes at ceiling, we report $\rho$ only where that denominator exceeds $0.02$, that is where $\overline{\mathrm{acc}}_{\text{Base}} < 0.98$, and print a dash otherwise. Pooled over $N \le 64$, it includes the short lengths where $\Delta \approx 0$, so it is conservative relative to the share removed at $N = 64$, which Section~\ref{sec:segmentation} reports separately. Open models use 20 to 80 trials per cell (Appendix~\ref{app:trials}); Gemini 3.5 uses 3 and Gemini 3.6 uses 5. We call a pooled cell significant at $|z| \ge 3.54$, the Bonferroni-corrected two-sided $p < 0.05$ across all 125 pooled cells we test. Those 125 are the 62 comparisons on unmodified tasks that Figure~\ref{fig:split} shows, the 48 cells of the two tokenization controls (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:controlcells}), and 15 reset-variant cells; keeping the reset cells in the family leaves the threshold conservative. Of the 125, the 91 that are open-model and non-reset are the subset the bootstraps below resample, since the Gemini and reset cells are not resampled.

\paragraph{Units of uncertainty.} A marker run and the no-marker run it is compared with see different random inputs from the same distribution, so comparisons are unpaired; a paired rerun at four models shows that pairing would have cut standard errors by 9\% to 22\% and moved no estimate by more than 0.01 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:paired}). The length, spacing, and alphabet cells are a fixed design rather than a sample, so the variance of the marker mean is taken over those strata rather than over pooled generations, which would charge the deterministic differences between cells to sampling noise. Writing $c$ for a (length, spacing, alphabet) stratum, $w_c$ for its share of the marker generations and $s_c^2 / n_c$ for the generation-level variance within it,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SE}(\Delta) = \sqrt{\sum_c w_c^2 \frac{s_c^2}{n_c} + \sum_N p_N^2\,\mathrm{SE}_{\text{Base},N}^2}, \qquad z = \Delta / \mathrm{SE}(\Delta),
\label{eq:se}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{SE}^2_{\text{Base},N}$ stratified over alphabets within each length $N$. This fixed-strata $z$ is the one we report throughout. It is larger than an unstratified generation-level $z$, which pools every generation of a cell as though the cells were drawn at random, in 44 of the 48 control cells; the cell-level bootstrap below is more conservative than either and is the second $z$ column of Appendix Table~\ref{tab:controlcells}. A bootstrap over generations changes the Bonferroni verdict in 1 of 91 cells; one over whole experimental configurations leaves 16 of 91 cells above the threshold instead of 62, so we read pooled significance as conditional on the grid we ran and rely on the length- and position-resolved analyses for the structure of the effects (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Redundant configurations.} At $A = 2$ the three position-local rules coincide: the only derangement of two symbols, the cyclic successor, and the conditional shift all flip the bit. At $M = 2$, \task{running\_sum\_local} computes \task{running\_xor}. These cells still test different \emph{instructions} for the same function, but they are not independent evidence across tasks; Appendix~\ref{app:stats} recomputes every pooled effect with them dropped, which moves the median $|\Delta|$ by 0.013, makes the effect larger in 54 of 91 cells, and leaves 23 of the 24 control cells at the four stronger models (Finding~2, Section~\ref{sec:segmentation}) significant. The other way the aggregate can be questioned is that it pools the short lengths where these models sit at ceiling and $\Delta \approx 0$. Restricting to $N \ge 32$ moves every count in the same direction: over the 28 dependency cells at the seven open models 11 are significantly positive and 3 negative, against 9 and 5 with the short lengths in, the median $|\Delta|$ rises from 0.030 to 0.043, and all 24 control cells at the four stronger models are significant with $\Delta$ between $+0.06$ and $+0.27$ rather than $+0.01$ and $+0.15$; dropping the redundant configurations as well gives 12 and 3 and a range of $+0.07$ to $+0.33$. Both restrictions therefore make the effects larger, so the pooled figure understates them rather than creating them. Figure~\ref{fig:split} acts on the first and not the second: it drops the redundant binary configurations, which are not independent across tasks, and keeps every length $N \in \{4, \dots, 64\}$, so that the aggregate still describes the grid we ran rather than its most favorable part. The per-cell tables, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:controlcells} among them, keep every alphabet and modulus. Two things follow that are worth stating plainly, because the convention is not uniform across the paper. First, the redundancy is a property of averaging \emph{across} tasks, not of any single task's row: Figure~\ref{fig:split} shows one row per task and so pools nothing across them, while the three-task model means behind Findings 1 and 2 do, and those keep the binary cells, which counts the shared bit-flip function once per task. Second, it does not matter much: recomputing the model-level $\rho$ of \Eqref{eq:share} over alphabets above two moves the headline range from 33\% to 83\% to 30\% to 84\%, and no model moves by more than 5 points. The 23 of 24 count above likewise holds under both conventions.

\paragraph{Per-position accuracy.} For a marker run with spacing $k$, output position $i$ (0-indexed, markers stripped) belongs to one of two classes,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}_k = \{\, i : i > 0,\ i \bmod k = 0 \,\} \ \text{(post-marker)}, \qquad \mathcal{W}_k = \{\, i : i > 0,\ i \bmod k \neq 0 \,\} \ \text{(within-segment)}.
\label{eq:classes}
\end{equation} Each class is compared with the no-marker run's accuracy at the same position indices, which controls for accuracy varying along the sequence. Per-position results (\Eqref{eq:classes}) pool $N \le 64$, $k \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$, and alphabet or modulus values; $k = 1$ has no within-segment positions, so per-position claims concern spacings of two or more. The unit for uncertainty is the generation, and intervals are 95\%.

And the letter gain is present even on four-letter strings ($+0.15$), where the controls show no marker effect at all.

\section{Tokenization-Independent Gains on Long Sequences}
\label{sec:segmentation}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_profile.pdf}
\caption{The marker effect (Bounds $-$ Base) at the four stronger models (Qwen2.5-14B and 32B, Qwen3.8-27B, Gemma-4-31B), mean over the three position-local tasks. (a) By sequence length, pooled over spacing. (b) By marker spacing at $N = 64$. Whiskers are 95\% intervals over the 12 model--task pairs; the three tasks show the same pattern. The two tokenization controls (spaced letters, digits) agree: no effect up to $N = 16$, a large effect at $N = 64$, and little dependence on spacing. Contiguous letters show a different signature: a gain at every length that is largest for the densest markers.}
\label{fig:profile}
\end{figure}

Holding each character to one token does not hold the whole prompt fixed: markers still add tokens, lengthen the sequence, and shift every later position. What the controls establish is narrower, that the gain is not an artifact of merging characters into multi-character tokens; we call it \emph{tokenization-independent} in that sense throughout. With tokenization fixed, markers still help the stronger models. With spaced letters the gain is $+0.06$ to $+0.13$ across the four, removing 38\% to 62\% of their remaining errors; with digits it is $+0.06$ to $+0.09$, removing 33\% to 83\%, where the share of remaining errors removed is $\rho$ of \Eqref{eq:share} over the model's three tasks (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:controlcells} gives every cell). Because these shares pool $N \le 64$, including the short lengths where $\Delta \approx 0$, they understate the share removed at $N = 64$. Leaving out \task{conditional\_shift}, whose digit rule is easier than its letter rule, raises rather than lowers them: that task has the lowest task-level share of the three at every one of these models and formats, so the two-task figure is higher however the tasks are combined (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). These effects are significant in 23 of the 24 cells at these four models under the paper's fixed-strata estimator, and in all 24 at $N = 64$ alone, where they run from $+0.07$ to $+0.37$; the three estimators are compared cell by cell in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}, and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:controlcells} lists every cell, pooled and at $N = 64$. At the three smallest models the gains are smaller and inconsistent, $-0.01$ to $+0.10$ across both controls, with 11 of these 18 cells significantly positive and one significantly negative (\task{cyclic\_shift} with digits at Mistral, $-0.05$), alongside the failure modes Appendix~\ref{app:models} documents at this scale. Gemini 3.6 Flash is at 0.94 to 1.00 on both controls without markers, so they leave it no room to show the effect: markers add $0.00$ to $+0.04$ there, none significant; in the one control cell where it is below 0.95 without markers, spaced letters at $N = 64$ (0.90), they add $+0.09$.

This benefit is a long-sequence effect (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}a; Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:grid} shows the whole length-by-spacing grid at every model). Up to $N = 16$, the four stronger models are near ceiling in both controls (0.92 to 0.96 without markers) and markers change nothing. At $N = 32$, accuracy without markers starts to fall and markers add $+0.07$ to $+0.09$; at $N = 64$, where accuracy without markers is 0.59 (spaced letters) and 0.68 (digits), markers add $+0.26$ and $+0.22$. In the strongest case, Qwen2.5-32B on \task{random\_permutation} with eight digits at $N = 64$, accuracy rises from 0.41 to between 0.88 and 0.99 with any spacing from every 2 to every 16 characters. The gain depends little on spacing (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}b): at $N = 64$ it is $+0.21$ to $+0.32$ for spaced letters and $+0.17$ to $+0.26$ for digits across all spacings, slightly weaker at the extremes (a marker after every character, which nearly doubles the number of output tokens, or after every 16).

The gain is spread over the whole segment rather than concentrated after the marker (Figure~\ref{fig:position}b). Scoring the controls the same way separates two readings of that gain: markers may stop the model losing its place, a gain an edit-distance alignment repairs away, or they may make each character more accurate, a gain alignment leaves standing. At $N = 64$ roughly half survives, and how much depends on the model: 53\% to 60\% at Qwen2.5-14B and 32B and 50\% to 56\% at Qwen3.8, but only 29\% and 38\% at Gemma 4, whose no-marker accuracy is already 0.75 to 0.79 index-scored and 0.89 to 0.91 aligned, so most of what markers buy it is place-keeping (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:alignedctl}). The two readings are therefore both right, in proportions that shift with how much room the model has left for substitution errors. A similar tokenization-independent gain appears inside segments on the dependency tasks, which use digits: within-segment positions gain $+0.08$ at Qwen2.5-32B, $+0.05$ at Gemma 4 and Gemini 3.5, $+0.03$ at Qwen3.8, and $+0.02$ at Gemini 3.6 over the no-marker run at the same positions, against $+0.01$ at Qwen2.5-14B. Under edit-distance alignment these within-segment gains shrink but stay positive in 11 of the 12 cells that are not \task{copy\_previous} at the four stronger models, so part of what markers do inside segments is keep the model from slipping out of alignment. The gap between the two scorings is the alignment-sensitive part of the gain: it is 52\% of the within-segment gain in the median cell, over the 11 of 16 dependency-task cells at the four stronger models that gain at least $+0.02$ and keep it under alignment (12 of the 16 gain at all). The remainder survives alignment and so reflects fewer substitutions, though the split between two scoring rules is a descriptive decomposition, not a causal one. Markers also change how errors cluster: at $N = 64$ under index scoring the probability that an error follows an error falls from 0.70--0.77 without markers to 0.24--0.59 with one every 2 to 16 characters, and the mean run of consecutive errors shortens from 3.1--3.9 positions to 1.3--2.3. Under alignment, where a slip no longer makes every later position wrong, that probability barely moves, so the large cut to the cascade is specific to index scoring, where it is phase slips that cascade. Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:aligned} and~\ref{tab:autocorr} give both comparisons cell by cell, and Appendix~\ref{app:perpos} reads them. 

The gain also extends beyond the lengths of our main grid: at Qwen2.5-32B, on \task{random\_permutation} with digits ($A = 8$, $k \in \{4, 16\}$), markers remove 89\% to 91\% of the remaining errors at $N = 64$, 78\% to 81\% at $N = 128$, and 56\% to 57\% at $N = 256$ (accuracy 0.18 without markers, 0.64 to 0.65 with), the ranges spanning \texttt{|}, commas, and line breaks as markers (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:delimiter}). One reading is that a marker every few characters helps a strong model keep its place in a long sequence; Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} states that hypothesis precisely and tests the simplest version of it. The experiments in this section establish the effect, not its cause. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} shows that the heads that attend most to markers carry little of this effect, and Qwen3.8-27B, whose layers are mostly linear attention, shows it comparably to the other strong models.

\section{The Post-Marker Blind Spot on Dependency Tasks}
\label{sec:blindspot}

Figure~\ref{fig:poscurve} shows the pattern along the sequence: at Qwen2.5-32B, on a position-local task every position gains, while on \task{running\_xor} the accuracy curve drops at exactly the positions that follow a marker and recovers at the next, and the same columns light up at every model. Figure~\ref{fig:examples} shows what this looks like in single generations: with a marker every four characters, the errors of an otherwise correct output sit at the position after each marker. On the four dependency tasks, with a marker every two or more characters, the position right after a marker is below the no-marker run at the same positions in 33 of 35 task--model cells (Figure~\ref{fig:position}c; these tasks use digits, so tokenization plays no role). The two exceptions are slight ($+0.02$ each, Qwen2.5-7B on \task{running\_xor} and Phi on \task{sliding\_window\_max}); every cell at Qwen2.5-14B and 32B, Qwen3.8, Gemma 4, and both Gemini versions shows the loss. We call this the \emph{blind spot} for short. It also shows up in how errors cluster: on these tasks sparse markers raise the chance of a fresh error after a correct position at three of the four stronger models (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:autocorr}), and that rise is the blind spot's own isolated errors, 20\% to 53\% of errors here being single errors between correct positions. Excluding pairs that touch a post-marker position brings it back to the no-marker level under edit-distance alignment, and at two of the four models under index scoring, while the drop in the chance that an error follows an error survives the exclusion. The term describes the behavior, not a claim about what the model represents: accuracy near chance shows that the information before the marker is not used correctly here, not that it is inaccessible to the model. 

The measured loss grows with how well the model does the task without markers, which at the small end is partly a floor effect: the three 7B-class models sit at 0.42 to 0.44 at these positions without markers, against a chance level near 0.40, so there is little room to fall. It is $-0.03$ to $-0.05$ at Phi, Mistral, and Qwen2.5-7B; $-0.13$ at Qwen2.5-14B and 32B; $-0.16$ at Qwen3.8; $-0.24$ at Gemma 4; and $-0.43$ and $-0.32$ at Gemini 3.5 and 3.6, which are near ceiling without markers. At the stronger models the post-marker position is close to chance: on \task{running\_xor} (chance 0.5) it is correct 0.51 to 0.55 of the time at Qwen2.5-14B and 32B, Qwen3.8, Gemma 4, and both Gemini versions. 

The failure is confined to the positions whose inputs lie across the marker. On the one-step tasks that is one position: on \task{running\_xor} at $k \ge 4$ the change against the no-marker run is $-0.15$ at the position right after a marker and $+0.20$ at the next, averaged over the four stronger models. \task{sliding\_window\_max} needs the two previous inputs, so the first \emph{two} positions after a marker cross it: pooled over $k \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$ at the four stronger models, the first changes by $-0.13$, the second by only $+0.03$, and the strictly internal positions gain $+0.08$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blindwindow}). Because both positions cross the marker, the per-position tables report the mean over the two as this task's post-marker value, which dilutes the first position's larger loss, and restrict its within-segment value to the strictly internal positions ($i \bmod k \ge 2$). At $k = 2$ every position of \task{sliding\_window\_max} is one of the two crossing positions, so the task has no strictly internal positions there and its within-segment entry is blank. Table~\ref{tab:blindwindow} splits the two apart. With a marker every 16 characters, accuracy one position later is back at 0.85 (Qwen2.5-32B), 0.70 (Qwen3.8), 0.82 (Gemma 4), 1.00 (Gemini 3.5), and 0.91 (Gemini 3.6). A marker after every character ($k = 1$) is different: there every position follows a marker, and on \task{copy\_previous} at $N = 32$ Qwen2.5-32B is more accurate with such markers (0.88) than without (0.83), plausibly because a fully regular pattern makes the lookback fixed (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\subsection{Robustness of the Blind Spot}
\label{sec:robust}

The checks in this subsection change how the effect is measured or delivered and leave the blind spot in place; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:summary} collects them.

\paragraph{Alignment-robust scoring.} Character accuracy compares characters index by index, so a dropped or inserted character misaligns everything after it. Re-scoring every run with an edit-distance alignment, which repairs such slips instead of counting the rest of the output as wrong, leaves the post-marker loss in place at every strong open model and task (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:aligned}). At Qwen2.5-32B it is $-0.24$ on \task{running\_xor} and $-0.22$ on \task{running\_sum\_local} under alignment, against $-0.19$ and $-0.16$ index by index.

\paragraph{Spacing, delivery, and state size.} The loss is a property of the position after a marker, not of the power-of-two rhythm the per-position analyses use. Taking $k = 2$, 3, and 4 separately gives mean post-marker changes of $-0.16$, $-0.18$, and $-0.15$ over the twenty dependency-task cells at the five open models, with $k = 3$ matching the sign of its neighbors in 17 of 20; moving every marker by $-1$, 0, or $+1$ from its periodic place, so the spacing is irregular, leaves the post-marker loss where it was or slightly deeper (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:jitterdep}); at Qwen2.5-32B, where we also ran $k = 5$ and 6, which share no divisor with that grid, the change stays between $-0.15$ and $-0.11$ across $k = 2$ to 6 ($-0.20$ to $-0.13$ on the three one-step tasks), without a trend while the within-segment change rises from $+0.02$ to $+0.12$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blindk}). Four further checks change nothing: delivering the prompt without the chat template or with other system prompts, raising the modulus of \task{running\_sum\_local} to 8 and 16, and sampling instead of greedy decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:delimiter}, Table~\ref{tab:decoding}).

\paragraph{Distinct ordered labels.} Every boundary so far carries the same symbol, so the position after one boundary cannot be told from the position after any other by the boundary itself: a model can only locate it by counting from the last marker. That suggests an obvious repair, and the one \emph{index hinting} uses \citep{zhou2024algorithms}: give each boundary a distinct label from an ordered set, so that it can be addressed. We test it on \task{running\_xor}, \task{running\_sum\_local} ($M = 4$) and the position-local control \task{random\_permutation} with digits, at Qwen2.5-32B and Gemma 4, $N \in \{32, 64\}$, $k = 4$, 20 trials, with the labels copied through to the output as the markers are (Appendix~\ref{app:marker}, Table~\ref{tab:ordered}). Three conditions separate the two readings of the effect: the original repeating \texttt{|}; a repeating \texttt{a}, which is a letter but still the same at every boundary; and \texttt{a}, \texttt{b}, \texttt{c}, \ldots\ in order, which is distinct and addressable. Lowercase letters do not collide with these tasks' digit vocabulary, unlike the bracketed reminder of Footnote~\ref{fn:reminder}.

Ordered labels do not remove the blind spot. In all eight dependency cells the post-marker change under ordered labels is within 0.04 of the change under the repeating bar ($-0.14$ against $-0.15$ on \task{running\_xor} at Qwen2.5-32B and $N = 64$; $-0.22$ against $-0.22$ at Gemma 4; $-0.42$ against $-0.45$ on \task{running\_sum\_local} at Gemma 4 and $N = 32$), and it is significant wherever the bar's is. This is not because the labels fail as markers: on those same cells they produce the within-segment gain the bar does ($+0.23$ against $+0.25$, $+0.23$ against $+0.24$, $+0.29$ against $+0.38$), so they segment the sequence and still leave the crossing position broken. A caveat runs the other way on the position-local control, where the gain is pure re-segmentation: there the letter-based markers recover much less of it at Qwen2.5-32B ($+0.08$ for ordered labels and $+0.01$ for the repeating letter, against $+0.58$ for the bar at $N = 64$), though at Gemma 4 they nearly match it ($+0.37$ and $+0.32$ against $+0.41$); an unfamiliar marker symbol can cost the segmentation gain at some models, as the symbol ablation of Appendix~\ref{app:marker} also found. So the blind spot is not caused by one boundary being mistaken for another: giving every boundary its own name leaves it where it was, which is what the attention and patching results of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predict, since what the marker disturbs is the lookback from one input to the one before it rather than the identification of the boundary. This is not the same as ruling out per-character addressing in the sense of index hinting \citep{zhou2024algorithms}, which labels every character rather than every boundary. We did not test that, and our own data suggests it would behave differently: at $k = 1$, where a marker sits between every pair of characters and every lookback therefore crosses the same one-marker gap, there is no blind spot at all on \task{copy\_previous} (0.88 against a no-marker 0.83 at Qwen2.5-32B, with the strongest lookback attention of any spacing; Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}d). The failure needs a boundary that interrupts some steps and not others.

\paragraph{Other delimiters and longer sequences.} At Qwen2.5-32B we replaced \texttt{|} with a comma or a line break, each with a constraint sentence naming it, and extended \task{running\_xor} and \task{running\_sum\_local} (modulus 4) to $N = 128$ and 256 ($k \in \{4, 16\}$, 20 trials per cell; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:delimiter}). With every delimiter and at every length, the post-marker position is at chance: 0.48 to 0.52 on \task{running\_xor} (chance 0.5) and 0.21 to 0.26 on \task{running\_sum\_local} (chance 0.25). Within-segment positions gain $+0.16$ to $+0.26$ and $+0.09$ to $+0.19$ over the no-marker run at the same positions. Measured against the no-marker run, the post-marker loss shrinks with length (on \task{running\_xor}, $-0.11$ to $-0.14$ at $N = 64$, $-0.03$ to $-0.05$ at 128, and $+0.03$ to $+0.05$ at 256), but only because the no-marker run itself decays toward chance on long sequences; the post-marker position does not improve. The blind spot is a property of the boundary, not of the symbol. The line break gives the largest pooled gain in all six dependency cells, by up to 0.08 over commas and 0.05 over the bar.

\paragraph{One-sided markers.} Markers in the input with an output asked for without them leave the post-marker position at chance on \task{running\_xor}; so does a delimiter forced into the output of an unmarked input, so on that task the loss is not specific to either side. On the other dependency tasks a one-sided condition lowers accuracy across the whole output rather than at the boundary (Section~\ref{sec:sides}).

\subsection{Repairing the Blind Spot}
\label{sec:repair}

Three interventions target the crossing step directly: changing the rule, changing the instruction, and finetuning. A fourth, making the needed input visible at the boundary, does not work in the one format we tried it in.\footnote{\label{fn:reminder}Making the needed input visible at the boundary is not enough in this format: repeating the digit before each marker in brackets after it (\texttt{0110|[0]1011}) at Qwen2.5-32B leaves the post-marker position at 0.55 on \task{running\_xor} and 0.31 on \task{running\_sum\_local} and lowers the within-segment positions (0.80 to 0.69 and 0.58 to 0.45), with 14\% to 24\% of outputs copying bracketed digits. The brackets intrude on a digit-only stream, so this does not rule out a less intrusive reminder.}

\paragraph{Reset variants.}
\label{sec:reset}
If the post-marker position fails because it needs the input before the marker, removing that need should repair exactly that position and leave the others alone. We rebuild each dependency task as a \emph{reset} variant: the position right after each marker is treated like the first position of the sequence, so the recurrence restarts at every marker. The prompt states the new rule with a worked example (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). Reset variants were run at Qwen2.5-7B and 32B, Qwen3.8-27B, and Gemma-4-31B.

Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:reset} confirms the prediction. On \task{running\_xor}, \task{running\_sum\_local}, and \task{sliding\_window\_max} at the three larger models, the post-marker position rises from 0.28--0.61 to 0.92--1.00, while within-segment positions change by $-0.09$ to $+0.07$. Qwen2.5-7B moves the same way with smaller gains (\task{running\_xor} 0.41 to 0.62; \task{running\_sum\_local} 0.22 to 0.49). Two caveats bound what this shows. The reset rule makes the post-marker position easier by design (on \task{running\_xor} it becomes ``copy this digit''), so the experiment localizes the harm rather than showing a new benefit. And the reset prompt alone shifts no-marker accuracy by $-0.08$ to $+0.06$, so a pooled effect there would compare a rebuilt rule against a baseline whose prompt never triggers it. We therefore do not report pooled reset effects at all; the comparison is the per-position one of Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:reset}, over marker runs alone.

\task{copy\_previous} behaves differently. Its post-marker position is also repaired (0.52--0.66 to 0.83--1.00), but its within-segment positions fall at Qwen3.8 ($-0.03$) and Gemma 4 ($-0.07$), and in the original task they are below the no-marker run at all nine models, both Gemini versions included (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:aligned}). Because the output is a shifted copy of the input, an off-by-one slip at a marker can carry through the rest of the segment; we read this as desynchronization, a failure distinct from the post-marker loss. Edit-distance alignment supports this reading: once slips are repaired, \task{copy\_previous}'s within-segment loss shrinks from $-0.26$ to $-0.03$ at Qwen3.8 and from $-0.11$ to $-0.05$ at Qwen2.5-14B, while its post-marker loss remains (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:aligned}).

\paragraph{Instruction wording.} Our instruction calls the bars ``structural boundaries'', which might invite the model to treat each segment as a fresh start. We reran the four dependency tasks at Qwen2.5-32B, Qwen3.8, and Gemma 4 with three other instructions (Table~\ref{tab:wording}): a \emph{neutral} one that only asks for each bar to be copied through; an \emph{across} one stating that the bars are only visual separators and that the position right after a bar uses the input right before it; and the across wording plus two worked examples with bars ($N = 8$, $k = 2$ and 4, produced by the task's own rule), the first annotated at the position right after its first bar. The neutral wording leaves the post-marker loss within 0.03 of the original in eleven of the twelve cells, the exception being \task{copy\_previous} at Gemma 4, which moves by 0.18. The across wording narrows it in all 12, most at Gemma 4, where it removes the loss outright on \task{copy\_previous} and \task{sliding\_window\_max}, and least at Qwen2.5-32B, where it moves it by at most 0.05. The worked examples add to the repair in 9 of 12 cells: at Gemma 4 most of it comes from the wording, while at Qwen2.5-32B the examples contribute about as much as the wording does. The position still stays below the no-marker run under every wording in 8 of the 12 cells, and at Qwen2.5-32B it remains near chance on \task{running\_xor} (0.57, against 0.70 without markers at the same positions). Within-segment gains and no-marker baselines move by at most 0.06, except on \task{copy\_previous}, whose within-segment positions the across wording lifts at Qwen3.8 and Gemma 4. At Qwen2.5-7B, near chance on these tasks, the worked prompt changes nothing except on \task{sliding\_window\_max} ($-0.09$ to $+0.02$). The framing does not create the blind spot, and naming the dependency, with worked examples of the crossing step, removes most of it at Gemma 4, about half at Qwen3.8, and about a third at Qwen2.5-32B: how far a model can be talked out of it depends on the model.

\paragraph{A finetuned separator.} Training can do what prompting only partly does. LoRA adapters trained on \task{running\_xor} and \task{running\_sum\_local} with marked answers as targets remove the blind spot entirely on those tasks at Qwen2.5-7B and 32B (post-marker accuracy 1.00), while controls trained on the same tasks without markers widen it. Whether that transfers is the question, and controls trained without markers for the same number of steps answer it: at 32B the only held-out repair specific to marker supervision is \task{sliding\_window\_max} (post-marker change $+0.05$, against $-0.11$ for the step-matched control), while on \task{copy\_previous} the step-matched control repairs the position nearly as well as the marked adapter does. The marked adapter also loses accuracy without markers on held-out tasks, but so does the step-matched control, so that cost comes from the amount of finetuning rather than from the markers. Marker supervision therefore looks like adaptation to the boundaries of the tasks it saw rather than a general rule for crossing a delimiter. Appendix~\ref{app:base} and Table~\ref{tab:lora} give the per-task numbers at both scales, including a shorter control that raises unmarked accuracy where the step-matched one lowers it.
\subsection{Prefix-State and External Tasks}

On tasks we did not design, the pattern holds; Gemini's own grid is Appendix Table~\ref{tab:gemini}. On CLRS-Text's running-minimum algorithm \citep{markeeva2024clrstext}, where every position depends on the whole prefix and markers go between multi-character items, so both the markers and the scoring are token-level, markers hurt wherever the model can do the task: $-0.20$ ($z = 4.8$) at Qwen2.5-32B and $-0.34$ ($z = 9.8$) on Gemini 3.5, while Qwen2.5-7B sits near the floor there (0.133 without markers) and gains $+0.05$. On ProcBench's per-character substitution task \citep{fujisawa2024procbench}, a position-local task, markers help, from 0.19 to 0.43 at Qwen2.5-7B ($+0.10$, $z = 6.7$) and from 0.39 to 0.76 at 32B ($+0.20$, $z = 10.8$); its strings mix letters and digits, so part of this gain may be tokenization. On string copying \citep{jelassi2024repeat}, where accuracy without markers is already 0.97 and 0.99, markers reach 1.00 at both scales but the gain stays below the significance threshold ($+0.009$ and $+0.007$, $z \le 2.0$).

Two further tasks we built with natural readings, the nesting depth of a parenthesis string and a running checksum, carry the whole prefix as state. At Qwen2.5-32B and Gemma 4 markers hurt both, and the within-segment positions fall with the post-marker position instead of gaining (Appendix~\ref{app:external}). The blind spot is confined to one position only when one position is all that needs what lies before the marker: with a one-step rule it is one position, with the three-symbol window two positions cross the marker though the loss sits on the first of them (a four-model mean of $-0.13$ against $+0.03$ for the second; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blindwindow}), and where the whole prefix is the state the within-segment positions fall with it. Because the other tables report this task's two crossing positions as one mean, its post-marker entries there are diluted toward zero, and a repair that brings that mean to zero need not have repaired the first position; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blindwindow} splits them.

\section{Real Sequences}
\label{sec:real}

Every input so far is drawn uniformly at random. Real sequences repeat symbols, have skewed frequencies, and contain words and motifs seen in pretraining. We therefore repeat the design on windows of two public corpora: human promoter DNA from the Nucleotide Transformer benchmark \citep{dallatorre2025nucleotide} (1,584 sequences of 300 bases, test split of its promoter task) and the WikiText-2 test split \citep{merity2017wikitext} reduced to lowercase letters (1,850 paragraphs). Each trial draws one window of $N \in \{16, 32, 64\}$ characters at a random offset, in the contiguous and the spaced format, with 20 trials per cell at Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B, and Gemma-4-31B. Each source has a position-local rule (the complementary base; each letter shifted by one), two one-step dependencies (whether a base is in the same chemical class as the previous one, with the purines \texttt{A} and \texttt{G} against the pyrimidines \texttt{C} and \texttt{T}, and copying the previous base; whether a letter comes later in the alphabet than the previous one, and copying the previous letter), and a prefix-state rule (a running count of \texttt{G} and \texttt{C} bases, or of the vowels \texttt{aeiou}, modulo 4). The first position has no predecessor, so unlike the synthetic \task{copy\_previous}, which copies it, the real copy-previous rules emit a dedicated start symbol there, \texttt{N} on DNA and \texttt{\#} on text, and the two comparison rules emit \texttt{0}; the prompts state this, and Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} gives them verbatim. The spaced cells at $N = 64$ were rerun with the $2N + 20$ budget, since a spaced answer can exceed $1.5N + 20$ tokens (Appendix~\ref{app:trials}), and Table~\ref{tab:real} reports those runs.

Four of these rules, the same-class, later-letter, and two counting rules, sit near or below chance without markers in the contiguous format at every model (0.04 to 0.52), where a marker effect would be uninterpretable; the analysis below therefore uses the position-local and copy-previous rules, which all three models do above chance in both formats. Where spacing lifts one of the four clear of chance it shows the synthetic pattern: on the same-class rule at Gemma 4 (0.67 without markers) and Qwen2.5-32B (0.63), and on the later-letter rule at Gemma 4 (0.63), the post-marker position changes by $-0.22$, $-0.13$, and $-0.13$ while the within-segment positions gain $+0.06$, $+0.03$, and $+0.05$ (Table~\ref{tab:real}, which carries every rule read here; the two counting rules, near chance in both formats, are in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:realfull}).

On the position-local and copy-previous rules, the synthetic results reappear. The position-local rules gain from markers at every model in the contiguous format, by $+0.12$ to $+0.21$ on DNA and $+0.15$ to $+0.26$ on text. As on letter strings (Section~\ref{sec:tokenization}), spacing the bases alone does at least as much as markers do, and more at two of the three models ($+0.17$ to $+0.23$), the post-marker advantage on contiguous DNA at the Qwen models ($+0.28$ and $+0.27$, against $+0.09$ and $+0.18$ within segments) disappears with spaced bases, and with spaced bases markers still help the two Qwen models ($+0.10$ and $+0.13$), while Gemma 4 is at 0.99 without them. The copy-previous rules lose at every model and in both formats, by $0.06$ to $0.39$ ($-0.06$ to $-0.39$ as signed changes), including where the model is near ceiling without markers (Qwen2.5-32B: 0.95 on DNA, 0.91 on text), and the post-marker position loses most. Gemma 4 restarts the rule at each marker: on DNA it writes the start symbol N, which the rule gives only at the first position, after 55\% of markers in the contiguous format and 90\% in the spaced format, and at the same positions in fewer than 0.1\% of no-marker outputs, so its post-marker accuracy falls to 0.17 and 0.05; on spaced text it writes the start symbol \# after 53\% of markers. The Qwen models restart after at most 13\% of markers; most of their post-marker errors are out of alignment, and their within-segment positions fall with the post-marker position. Rescoring these runs under edit-distance alignment, as for the synthetic tasks, separates the two: over the 12 copy-previous cells (two sources, three models, two formats) alignment removes a median 72\% of the within-segment drop (range 24\% to 92\%, every cell shrinking toward zero), taking it from $-0.23$ to $-0.06$ on average, while the post-marker drop largely survives it ($-0.43$ to $-0.32$, a median 21\% removed, and in three cells it grows). The within-segment loss on real sequences is therefore mostly desynchronization after a genuine error at the marker, not degradation spread through the segment. One qualification: on the position-local real tasks alignment also removes about half of the within-segment \emph{gain}, so it compresses within-segment differences on these sequences generally, and the attribution is directional rather than specific to copy-previous (Table~\ref{tab:real}). The copy-previous cells therefore combine the blind spot with that desynchronization and show no within-segment gain; the spaced one-step rules above, where the models are above chance, show the gain alongside the post-marker loss, as the synthetic dependency tasks do. On real DNA and text, then, a position-local rule gains from markers and a dependency that crosses one fails after it.

\section{Mechanistic Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The interventions here are narrower than the rest of the paper: mostly at Qwen2.5-32B, head ablations mainly on \task{random\_permutation}, and no interventions on MLPs or paths. They bound what marker-attending heads contribute and locate where the blind spot acts on the prediction; they are not a full account.

\paragraph{Working hypothesis.} To produce output position $i$, a model must find input position $i$ (and, on dependency tasks, position $i - 1$) among many near-identical tokens. We hypothesize that markers turn this global alignment into a local one: with a marker every $k$ characters, the model can keep its place by the markers it has passed and its offset since the last one. This would explain a gain that appears only on long sequences, is spread over the whole segment, depends little on $k$, and shrinks when alignment slips are forgiven in scoring. The same local frame would explain the blind spot: if ``the previous input'' is looked up relative to the current segment, the first position of a segment finds a marker where its predecessor should be. This is a conjecture, not a result: the experiments below test only its simplest version, in which a few marker-attending heads carry the effects, and they rule that version out.

\subsection{Attention-Head Ablations}

\paragraph{Marker-attending heads.} In Qwen2.5, individual heads attend sharply from each position to the nearest marker (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}b). Heads are ranked by the mean attention mass that all query positions place on the marker tokens, computed per (layer, head) over five held-out trials at the same task, length, and spacing and re-identified for every such cell; the top $n$ are taken across all layers. Qwen2.5-32B has 2,560 heads (64 layers of 40), so the ten are 0.4\% of them and the 200 are 7.8\%. For the ten heads with the most attention to markers at Qwen2.5-32B, the mean percentile on a previous-token test is 95 and on an induction-head test 31; since heads selected for attending to an adjacent token will score high on a previous-token test, we read this only as ``these are local heads''. Those ten, identified on one cell, lie in layers 1 to 37 of 64; the sets that Table~\ref{tab:ablation} ablates are re-identified per cell and span layers 1 to 63, so the selection is not confined to one depth; heads are ranked by attention to markers rather than by causal effect, and we did not search for the heads with the largest marginal effect, so the layer profile of Section~\ref{sec:patching} is the only causal layerwise localization we offer. The total attention mass on markers rises with density, but so does the number of markers, so we do not claim markers are attention sinks in the sense of drawing more attention than content tokens on average across query positions; at the step right after a marker they do draw more attention than the input that step needs (Section~\ref{sec:patching}).

\paragraph{Blocking the ten most marker-attending heads.} We block the ten most marker-attending heads from the markers during generation and compare with two controls (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The block acts after the softmax: in each blocked head, the attention weights from every query position to the marker tokens are set to zero and each row is renormalized, so the head's remaining attention is redistributed over non-marker tokens and nothing else in the forward pass changes; a self-test at the start of every run confirms that the blocked weights are exactly zero. Random heads are drawn uniformly from all (layer, head) pairs outside the top set, not matched by layer, though their median layer is close to the top heads' (34 against 30 for ten heads). On digits at Qwen2.5-32B, where markers raise accuracy from 0.41 to 0.96 at $N = 64$, blocking removes 1\% of the gain (paired drop of $0.005 \pm 0.009$). At $N = 64$ it removes 13\% on spaced letters (paired drop of $0.065 \pm 0.058$) and 19\% on contiguous letters (paired drop of $0.049 \pm 0.030$), against at most 5\% for either control; at $N = 32$ it removes 7\% or less in every format. On spaced letters part of this is one trial ($N = 64$, $k = 8$): in it the 10-head runs and the 50-head controls all close the first segment one character early and then copy the rest correctly but shifted by one, which index scoring counts as 54 errors, while the unblocked run, the top-50 run, and every 200-head run get it right. Without that trial, blocking the ten heads removes 9\% on spaced letters and the controls at most 1\%. The heads that attend most to markers thus carry about none of the gain on digits, a modest and uncertain share on spaced letters, and up to a fifth on contiguous letters. At Qwen2.5-7B, marker gains in this 20-trial head-ablation subset are small and the drops from blocking are within the range of the controls (for example, $+0.03 \pm 0.05$ on spaced letters at $N = 32$, against $+0.03$ for random heads). Blocking ten heads is a narrow intervention, and the benefit may be distributed; what we can say is that it is not concentrated in the heads that attend to markers most.

\paragraph{Head-count dose response.} At $N = 64$ and $k = 8$, against $k \in \{2, 8\}$ pooled in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, blocking the 50 most marker-attending heads removes 1\% to 4\% of the gain, against 7\% for its own controls on spaced letters, which is entirely the single phase-slip trial described above and 0\% without it, and the top 200 (7.8\% of the model's heads) about a quarter, 28\% on spaced letters and 21\% on digits, while 200 random heads blocked from the markers, or the top 200 blocked from as many random non-marker tokens, remove nothing (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}c). The benefit runs partly through attention to the markers, but thinly spread over the heads this ranking finds: even 200 of them carry about a quarter of it. The ranking is by how much attention a head places on markers, not by its causal contribution, and attention mass marks a passive sink as readily as a working head, so a circuit that a causal ranking would find concentrated is not excluded.

\paragraph{Attention to the needed input.} On \task{copy\_previous} at $N=32$, a marker after every character keeps the marker pattern aligned with the one-step lookback, and attention from each output position to the input it needs is high (0.535). At $k=2$, every other lookback crosses a marker; attention to the needed token falls to 0.101 and accuracy drops from 0.88 to 0.65, below the no-marker baseline of 0.83, on the 40-generation batch these attention runs use (0.85, 0.63 and 0.81 over the pooled grid). At $k=8$ both recover (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}d). The attention here is the mean over the ten heads, chosen separately at each spacing, that attend most to the needed input, averaged over all output positions; part of the recovery at $k = 8$ is therefore that only one position in eight follows a marker, against every other position at $k = 2$, and Section~\ref{sec:patching} isolates the post-marker step. This is the attention-level counterpart of the blind spot.

\paragraph{A hybrid-attention model.} Qwen3.8-27B, in which 48 of 64 layers use Gated DeltaNet linear attention \citep{yang2025gated} rather than softmax attention, shows the tokenization-independent gain and the blind spot comparably to the other strong models.

\subsection{Input Versus Output Markers}
\label{sec:sides}

The intervention changes two things at once: it segments the input, and it asks for a segmented
output. To separate them we ran a $2 \times 2$ at Qwen2.5-32B on \task{running\_xor} and
\task{random\_permutation} with digits ($N \in \{32, 64\}$, $k \in \{4, 16\}$, 20 trials),
varying whether the input carries markers and whether the output is asked to. The three marker-bearing conditions are built from the same underlying string per trial, so they are paired. Writing $A_{io}$ for accuracy with ($i = 1$) or without ($i = 0$) markers in the input and with ($o = 1$) or without ($o = 0$) markers in the output, the interaction
\begin{equation}
I = A_{11} - A_{10} - A_{01} + A_{00}
\label{eq:interaction}
\end{equation}
is positive when marking both sides helps more than the two single-sided changes add up to.

Asking for the format through the prompt alone is confounded by partial compliance: asked to omit the markers from its output, the model still emits them in 28\% to 62\% of generations, and asked to insert them into an unmarked output, it often does not (on \task{random\_permutation} it never produced the requested placement). The interaction there is already positive ($+0.16$ and $+0.14$, both intervals excluding zero), but the clean test constrains the decoder.

We reran the same four cells with a logits processor that
counts the answer characters as they are generated and either forces the marker token when one is due or removes every marker-bearing token from consideration, so that the output format is constrained by the decoder rather than requested solely through the prompt (Table~\ref{tab:sidesforced}). At each step it decodes the answer so far, counts its non-marker characters $c$, and, when the condition calls for markers and $c$ is a positive multiple of $k$, masks every token except the marker; otherwise it masks every token whose decoded string contains a bar; once $c$ reaches $N$ it stops intervening, which is why an overrun past the answer can cost full-output compliance without touching the scored first $N$ characters. The same machinery runs in all four cells, so the comparison is between conditions and not between a constrained and an unconstrained decoder. Decoding is greedy without beam search and the mask only removes tokens, so at every step the chosen token is the model's own highest-scoring allowed token; the mask cannot change the scores of the tokens it leaves. The constraint holds in 99\% to 100\% of full outputs, and in every scored first-$N$ answer, in the
no-marker, input-only, and both-sides cells; in the output-only cell the whole output has the requested format in 64\% to 75\% of
generations on these two tasks, but within the first $N$ answer characters, the part that is scored, it is exact in every generation: the shortfall is markers written after the answer, while the model keeps generating. Constraining does
not itself move the results: the no-marker and both-sides cells reproduce their unconstrained
counterparts to within 0.03.

Under constrained decoding, on these two tasks at Qwen2.5-32B, \emph{neither side alone helps, and both hurt}. Markers in the input
with an output genuinely free of them give $-0.03$ on \task{running\_xor} and $-0.15$ on
\task{random\_permutation}; markers forced into the output of an unmarked input give $-0.05$ and
$-0.16$. Only the combination helps, by $+0.10$ and $+0.39$. The interaction is $+0.19$ (95\%
interval $[0.15, 0.24]$) and $+0.70$ ($[0.61, 0.79]$). The input-only cell under enforcement scores 0.40 on \task{random\_permutation}, close to
the 0.42 of the unenforced generations that happened to comply. On the tasks and model tested, a marker helps only when it appears on both sides, where it can align the output being written with the input being read; segmenting the input while writing an unsegmented output is worse than not segmenting at all. Restricted to the output-only generations whose whole output has the requested format, a selected and easier subset, accuracy is 0.62 on \task{running\_xor} and 0.45 on \task{random\_permutation}, still below the no-marker cell, and the interaction stays positive ($+0.18$ and $+0.64$). Two more dependency tasks at Qwen2.5-32B, \task{copy\_previous} and \task{running\_sum\_local} ($M = 4$), repeat the pattern (Table~\ref{tab:sidesforced}): input alone $-0.12$ and $-0.04$, output alone $-0.06$ and $-0.05$, both sides $+0.02$ and $+0.08$, with interactions of $+0.20$ ($[0.12, 0.27]$) and $+0.17$ ($[0.13, 0.22]$), so the interaction holds on all four tasks tested at this model. On these two tasks the whole output has the output-only format in only 32\% to 35\% of generations, because the model runs past the required length and keeps inserting markers, but the scored first $N$ characters are exact in every generation (Table~\ref{tab:sidesforced}). The fully compliant subset scores 0.80 on \task{copy\_previous} and 0.41 on \task{running\_sum\_local}, 0.03 and 0.02 above the no-marker cell, and the interaction computed with it stays positive ($+0.11$ on both).

The output-only cell shows that the loss at the crossing position is not a property of the input side. Read as raw accuracy at the crossing position against the no-marker run at the same indices, every marker-bearing cell is worse on \task{running\_xor}: 0.53 with markers in the input and an output enforced to carry none, 0.53 with an unmarked input and a delimiter forced into the output every $k$ characters, and 0.51 with markers on both sides, against 0.69 without markers. But these cells also differ in overall accuracy, so the sharper question is whether a cell loses more at the crossing position than elsewhere in its own output. The last column of Table~\ref{tab:sidesforced} answers it, differencing each cell's post-marker minus within-segment accuracy against the no-marker cell's (10,000-sample percentile bootstrap). By that test the deficit is specific to the crossing position in all three marker cells of \task{running\_xor} ($-0.15$ input-only, $-0.12$ output-only, $-0.34$ both sides, every interval excluding zero); on \task{copy\_previous} and \task{running\_sum\_local} only the both-sides cell shows one ($-0.17$ and $-0.16$), the one-sided cells there losing accuracy evenly across the output rather than at the boundary; and on \task{random\_permutation}, which is position-local and has no dependency to break, no cell shows one. Two things follow. An output delimiter alone reproduces the failure on \task{running\_xor}, which fits Section~\ref{sec:patching}, where the step after a marker is queried at the output bar and the lookback band is measured there. And the position-specific deficit is largest in exactly the condition that helps most overall, with markers on both sides, which is the condition the rest of the paper runs: the gain of Finding~2 and the loss of Finding~3 are produced together.

\subsection{Activation Patching and Cross-Boundary Attention}
\label{sec:patching}

Where is the blind spot represented? We teacher-force Qwen2.5-32B on \task{running\_xor} and \task{running\_sum\_local} ($M = 4$; $N = 32$, $k = 4$, 20 inputs per task) with and without markers, read the log-odds of the correct digit against the reset digit at each post-marker step where the two differ (69 of 140 such positions on \task{running\_xor}, 100 of 140 on \task{running\_sum\_local}; averaged within an input, then over inputs), and then overwrite the marked run's residual stream at one layer, or at every layer, with the no-marker run's at chosen positions (Appendix~\ref{app:patching}, Table~\ref{tab:patching}). Without patching the marked run prefers the reset digit by 6 to 7 nats; the gap between the clean and the marked run's log-odds, the denominator of every recovery ratio, is 7 to 9 nats. Recovery at a site is
\begin{equation}
R = \frac{\ell_{\text{patched}} - \ell_{\text{marked}}}{\ell_{\text{clean}} - \ell_{\text{marked}}},
\label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the log-odds of the correct digit against the reset digit, so $R = 0$ is the marked run and $R = 1$ the clean run.
Patching every input digit recovers 75\% and 86\% of the gap, the input digit right after the marker alone 59\% and 64\%, the digit before the marker 12\% and 1\%, and the answer digit already written 18\% and 20\%. Patched one layer at a time, the recovery from the input digits peaks at 81\% and 94\% around layer 34 of 64 and is gone by layer 52, and at Qwen3.8 little is left after layer 54. Patching thus identifies the residual-stream representation of the first input digit after a marker as the most effective site at which to repair the failure, which is not the same as showing the failure resides there: that token is the one the output bar still attends to, and in the clean donor it has already absorbed the preceding input through early local heads, so patching it can deliver the missing operand rather than correct a corrupted representation. Argmax accuracy recovers by at most 29\% even where the log-odds recover 75\% to 86\%, because the patched log-odds end near zero rather than clearly positive. That site has its strongest single-layer recovery around layer 34 of 64, with little after layer 52; because the clean and marked runs differ in token positions and context, patching shows where the difference acts, not where it originates. The same procedure at Gemma 4 (60 layers) and Qwen3.8 (64 layers, mostly linear attention) gives the same ordering of sites: every input digit recovers 61\% and 60\% of the gap at Gemma 4 and 46\% and 74\% at Qwen3.8, the input digit after the marker alone 48\% and 30\% at Gemma 4 and 48\% and 69\% at Qwen3.8, the digit before the marker at most 6\% at Gemma 4 and 21\% at Qwen3.8, and the answer already written 15\% to 20\% at Gemma 4 and 8\% to 35\% at Qwen3.8; the single-layer peak of the every-input curves is around layer 26 of 60 at Gemma 4 and layers 25 to 34 of 64 at Qwen3.8, after which Gemma 4's curve is below 0.05 from layer 44 while Qwen3.8's holds 0.05 to 0.11 as far as layer 58 (Appendix~\ref{app:patching}). Qwen3.8's clean--marked gap is 2.2 and 2.3 nats, so its ratios are the least precise of the three. At Qwen2.5-7B, at chance on these tasks at $N = 32$ even without markers, the gap is too small to patch (Appendix~\ref{app:patching}).

\paragraph{Caveats of the cross-run comparison.} Two features of the comparison qualify it: in the marked run the step that predicts the answer after a marker is read at the output bar rather than at the previous answer digit, so patching input tokens or the answer digit written before the marker leaves that bar's own residual stream, the generating query, untouched, and every patched token sits at a later absolute position than its clean counterpart, so a single patched token breaks positional continuity with its neighbors. Patching every input restores that continuity within a segment, but not across one: the marker still stands between the two inputs, so a pair one apart in the clean run is two apart in the marked run, and every patched token keeps an offset to the generating query. That residual distortion is one reason the log-odds recover far more than the argmax does. To test whether either feature produced the result, we repeated the experiment with a donor that has neither: the across-wording marked run of Section~\ref{sec:repair}, which carries the same input, the same ground-truth answer and markers in the same places, and whose instruction states that the position after a bar uses the input before it. Every relative distance among input and answer tokens is then identical in the two runs, only the instruction prefix differs in length (a constant 33 tokens, which relative attention is insensitive to), and the step after a marker is read at the output bar in both, so the query token is the same. That donor is itself only partly repaired, preferring the reset digit by 2.9 and 3.3 nats against the recipient's 7.0 and 6.1, so the gap to recover across is 4.2 and 2.8 nats rather than 7 to 9 and the fractions below are not comparable in size with those above. Their ordering is what matters, and it is the same: every input digit recovers 34\% and 37\%, the input right after the marker alone 18\% and 28\%, the input before it 9\% and 4\%, and the answer already written 10\% and 10\%, with the single-layer peaks at layers 38 to 43 rather than 34 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:patchaligned}). The post-marker input is again the dominant site by a factor of two to seven over the others, so neither the index shift nor the query-token difference is what identified it. With a position-aligned donor the generating query can also be patched, which the no-marker comparison cannot do, and it answers where the across-wording repair lives. Patching the output bar's own residual stream recovers the whole gap, but that is forced rather than informative: the logits are read at that position, so overwriting it at every layer transplants the donor's readout, and adding the post-marker input to it changes nothing (103\% either way). The per-layer profile is the result. Patching the bar does nothing through the first two thirds of the network (0.01 at layer 32), reaches 0.16 to 0.20 at layer 40 and 0.83 to 0.89 by layer 48, and saturates after that, whereas the input sites peak at layers 37 to 40. The query state acquires the answer downstream of where the difference between the runs acts, which is what one would expect if the crossing step is computed from the input representations and only then written into the position that reads them out. Without markers the input before and after the marker position are adjacent, so early local heads can already have copied the earlier one into the later one's representation, a step the bar between them interrupts; this is a further reason the input after the marker is the main patching site.

\paragraph{Attention to the input before the marker.} Patching shows which representation the failure acts through; the attention at the failing step shows what the model reads. In the same teacher-forced runs at Qwen2.5-32B we record, for the step that writes each answer character, the attention placed on the input character before it, per layer and head (Appendix~\ref{app:patching}, Table~\ref{tab:attention}). Without markers, a band of heads in layers 35 to 45 looks back at that character, the strongest of them with 0.22 to 0.60 of its attention (Figure~\ref{fig:mechlayers}c, d). With markers the band is intact at within-segment steps, which keep 89\% to 96\% of the unmarked lookback in the band of layers 35 to 45, and gone at the step after a marker, which keeps 30\% to 38\% of it on average over heads and 23\% to 27\% for the strongest head (the same totals restricted to layers 35 to 45 are 90\%, 26\% to 37\% and 20\% to 25\%); in layers 35 to 45 the strongest head's attention to the needed input falls to 0.04 to 0.07. At that step the marker receives 0.56 to 0.59 of the attention, summed over the 64 layers (so out of a possible 64) and averaged over heads, more than the 0.44 to 0.46 the needed input receives without markers. This is why patching the input before the marker recovers little: the step after a marker barely reads it, while its attention to the input after the marker stays high (0.67 and 0.98; Appendix~\ref{app:patching} discusses the comparison's caveats). Single-layer patching recovers the prediction only up to the layers of this band (Figure~\ref{fig:mechlayers}a, b): once the lookback has run, replacing the input's representation no longer helps. The same collapse appears at Gemma 4 and Qwen3.8, where attention from the step after a marker to the input before it falls from 0.47 to 0.57 in the clean run to 0.08 to 0.13 (Table~\ref{tab:attention}). In these runs, however, the step after a marker is read at the output bar, so the bar is also the query. With markers in the input only, the query is the previous answer digit, and the lookback largely survives: the input before the marker keeps a layer-summed attention of 0.37 to 0.40 at Qwen2.5-32B (against 0.44 to 0.46 clean), 0.30 to 0.33 at Gemma 4, and 0.40 to 0.42 across the 16 softmax layers of Qwen3.8, whose sums run over those layers alone rather than over 60 or 64, so they are not comparable across models, while the input at the step's own position falls to 0.19 to 0.35 (against 0.59 to 0.78 clean) and the marker takes 0.40 to 0.52. Marking the output alone settles which side carries the collapse. With an unmarked input and a marked answer, so that the step is read at the output bar but no marker stands between $x_{j-1}$ and $x_j$, the lookback falls to 0.18 and 0.24, close to the 0.17 and 0.14 of the both-sides run and far from the 0.40 and 0.37 that input markers alone leave (Table~\ref{tab:attention}). The bar in the output is therefore sufficient to collapse the lookback, and the bar in the input is not necessary for it; what the input marker does instead is take attention away from $x_j$, which is the separate effect \task{running\_xor} is sensitive to. The two experiments agree on the same token: the input right after the marker is both the dominant patching site (59\% and 64\% of the gap recovered at Qwen2.5-32B, Table~\ref{tab:patching}) and the token the marker takes attention away from even once the output bar is gone, which is why \task{running\_xor}, which needs $x_{j-1}$ and $x_j$ together, still shows a boundary-specific deficit under input markers alone, while on \task{copy\_previous} and \task{running\_sum\_local} a deficit relative to the rest of the segment appears only with markers on both sides (Section~\ref{sec:sides}). That contrast should not be read as the crossing step surviving on \task{running\_sum\_local}, which needs both operands too: there the one-sided conditions push the post-marker position to 0.28 and 0.30 against a chance level of 0.25, and pull the within-segment positions down with it, to 0.35 and 0.34 from 0.39 without markers, so the two classes fall together and the difference between them stays small. The collapse of the lookback is therefore tied to the output bar, and the input-only failure on \task{running\_xor} goes with attention drawn from the current input to the marker rather than with a lost lookback; neither measurement shows which carries the dependency.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:practical}
\label{sec:limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The experiments separate three things a content-free marker does. On letter strings it mostly re-tokenizes; with one token per character it still helps the stronger models keep their place on long sequences; and with a marker every two or more characters, the position right after it fails whenever its output depends on the input before the marker, while rewriting the rule so that position needs nothing before the marker repairs exactly that position. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:summary} collects the main effect sizes.

\paragraph{Practical guidance.} Table~\ref{tab:decision} gives the recommendations scenario by scenario; two operational trade-offs behind it are worth stating. The first is cost in tokens and time. Spacing characters is cheaper than marking them (48 spaced letters are 48 tokens, against 95 with a marker after every letter), and sparse markers can be cheaper still than no markers at long lengths, because they stop models running past the required length: on contiguous-letter tasks at $N = 64$ to 128, 18\% to 95\% of outputs overrun without markers at Qwen2.5-32B, Qwen3.8, and Gemma 4, against 0\% to 13\% with a marker every 8 characters, so on contiguous \task{cyclic\_shift} at Qwen2.5-32B a marker every 4 or 8 characters takes less time than none from $N = 64$ on (0.66 times as long at $N = 64$ and 0.44 at $N = 128$ for $k = 8$), while $k = 1$ costs more at every length and $k = 2$ up to $N = 64$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:wallclock}). Throughput is flat at 14 to 15 tokens per second throughout, so that saving is the baseline's runaway generation being cut short, not faster decoding: under a hard stop after $N$ characters, markers add $\lfloor N/k \rfloor$ tokens and cost a little more; we did not time the one-token-per-character formats. The second trade-off is against splitting the work up: true divide-and-conquer, one call per segment, holds accuracy nearly flat (0.93 at $N = 8$ to 0.92 at 128 on contiguous \task{cyclic\_shift}) but costs about three times the wall-clock of a single marked call. Markers buy about 60\% of that stability within one call. Where an output depends on the input just before a marker, the marker should not be placed there, or the rule should be restated so that the position after each marker needs nothing before it.

\paragraph{The marker symbol and its placement.} On contiguous-letter \task{cyclic\_shift} at two models, the choice of symbol matters less than its familiarity (Appendix~\ref{app:marker}): \texttt{;} and \texttt{\#} keep accuracy above the no-marker baseline as \texttt{|} does, while a reserved special token the model has never seen in this role falls far below it, and the benefit reverses at Qwen2.5-32B under a terse cipher-style phrasing of the instruction. Moving every marker by up to one position leaves the blind spot in place on the dependency tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:marker}, Table~\ref{tab:jitterdep}), and commas and line breaks behave like \texttt{|} there, so neither effect depends on exact periodicity or on this particular symbol (Appendix~\ref{app:marker}).

\paragraph{Mechanism.} The experiments bound the mechanism rather than settle it. Marker-directed attention contributes to the gain, but thinly: blocking 200 marker-attending heads removes about a quarter of it and fewer heads about nothing. Those heads are ranked by attention mass rather than causal effect, and we did not test MLP or path-level interventions, so a more concentrated circuit found another way is not ruled out. Marking only one side hurts and only marking both helps; the deficit specific to the crossing position appears with markers on both sides on all three dependency tasks, and with either side alone on \task{running\_xor}, so it is not a property of the input side. The failure is consistent with the model resolving ``the previous input'' within the current segment: with markers on both sides, the heads that read the previous input stop reading it at the step after a marker at all three models we measured, and patching locates the failure in the representation of the first input after the marker; with input markers only, the lookback survives but the current input loses attention to the marker, so the attention measurements do not isolate a single cause, and two tasks at three models do not establish the mechanism in general. Separators that act as attention anchors help sequence-level questions over long numerical inputs \citep{sun2026sepseq}; for per-position transformations the anchor helps only when the output is segmented with the input, and it is also where the cross-boundary dependency is lost. Combining patching with causal head gating \citep{nam2025headgating}, which names the heads involved, is the natural next probe. Retokenization sampling \citep{jain2026retokenization} finds that behavior stays sensitive to how the same bytes are segmented deep into the network; our controls show that a segmentation imposed by content-free tokens, with the per-character tokenization fixed, persists in the same way.

\paragraph{Limitations: scope and model coverage.} The open models are instruction-tuned, five of them 2024-era; base checkpoints of Qwen2.5-7B and 32B show the blind spot at close to its instruct size (Appendix~\ref{app:base}), so instruction tuning enlarges the effects but does not create them. Apart from the real windows of Section~\ref{sec:real}, the tasks are synthetic character-level rules on letters or digits, with alphabets or moduli of at most eight symbols in the core grids (32 in one follow-up), dependencies of one or two positions, and lengths up to $N = 64$ (256 in one follow-up); other tokenizer families, non-Latin scripts, and thousands of characters are untested. The open models are decoded greedily; Gemini 3.6 ignores the temperature parameter, so its runs are samples, though across its five trials per cell the post-marker change on the dependency tasks stays between $-0.27$ and $-0.37$ (pooled $-0.32$, standard deviation 0.04); Gemini 3.5 has 3 trials per cell, the two were queried three months apart, Gemini's tokenization is known only through API token counts, and at 0.94 to 1.00 without markers on the controls Gemini gives no test of the tokenization-independent gain. Symbol, wording, placement, and delimiter ablations cover few tasks and models. We test only content-free markers and only rotary position embeddings; ALiBi or FIRE encodings, separator tokens such as \texttt{[SEP]}, applied settings such as log lines or tables, and byte-level models are effectively untested: the one openly available byte-level chat model, EvaByte-SFT \citep{zheng2025evabyte}, does not reach the lengths at which the blind spot is measured (Appendix~\ref{app:base}).

\paragraph{Methodological caveats.} Position-wise scoring penalizes a dropped character at every later position, which alignment-robust scoring corrects without removing the blind spot (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:aligned}); marker and no-marker runs use different inputs, at a small cost in precision (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:paired}); the no-marker baseline keeps the sentence describing the markers, at a measured cost of well under 0.01 in accuracy (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}); the reset rule makes the post-marker position easier by construction; and per-position claims concern spacings of two or more.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} In sum, a content-free marker is a cheap, single-call intervention whose benefit and whose failure mode are consistent with one explanation, that it gives the model a local frame, although the experiments establish the two effects more firmly than how the model uses the boundaries. Separate the characters, mark long sequences sparsely on both input and output, and keep markers away from any position whose output depends on what came just before them.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Code, prompts, raw generations, and analysis scripts are at \url{https://github.com/faezehkhazaee97/AvalancheBreak}. All inputs are generated from deterministic seeds, and every prompt can be regenerated from (task, format, length, spacing, alphabet, trial).

\section*{Broader Impact Statement}

This is a measurement study of a prompting convention, and we see no significant risk of harm from it. One finding bears on practice. Inserting delimiters into a model's input is a common formatting habit, and we show that it makes the position right after a delimiter fail whenever the output there depends on what came before it. The failure is quiet: the output keeps its shape and its length, the surrounding positions stay accurate, and a pipeline that checks only the format will not notice. Punctuation, line breaks, and field separators all act this way, so the effect can reach text that was never deliberately marked. Anyone splitting a sequence before a model reads it, in text processing or over biological sequences, should test the positions next to the boundaries rather than the output as a whole. Section~\ref{sec:practical} gives the placement rules that avoid it. We release code, prompts, and raw generations so the failure can be checked for directly.

\paragraph{\task{cyclic\_shift} ($N = 8$, $A = 4$, $k = 4$).}
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
You are a deterministic sequence transformer.
Task: Cyclic character shift.
Alphabet: A, B, C, D
Rule: Replace every character with the next one in the alphabet.
Mapping: D -> A, and A -> B.
Constraint: The input contains vertical bars (|). These are structural
boundaries. DO NOT shift them. Copy them exactly to the output.
No spaces, no explanations.

Input: CABA|CCDA
Output:                 (expected: DBCB|DDAB)
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}

\paragraph{Spaced and digit formats.} The spaced version of this cell shows the input as \texttt{C A B A | C C D A} and replaces ``No spaces, no explanations.'' with ``Write the output in the same format as the input: every character separated by a single space. No explanations.''; everything else is unchanged. The digit version relabels $A, B, C, D$ as $0, 1, 2, 3$ (input \texttt{2010|2230}), with the task line, rule, and mapping worded for digits (``Cyclic digit shift'', ``Replace every digit with the next one in the alphabet''). The same seed draws the same positions in all three formats.

\paragraph{\task{running\_xor}, original and reset ($N = 8$, $k = 4$).} Both prompts state the rule $y_i = x_i \oplus x_{i-1}$ with the first digit copied; the lines the reset variant adds are in the diff list below, beside the other three wordings, so all five variants can be compared in one place. \paragraph{\task{sliding\_window\_max} ($N = 8$, $k = 4$).} The prompt states the rule with a worked example and adds: \emph{``DO NOT include them [the bars] in the window or count them as digits.''} For input \texttt{5170|1247} the expected output is \texttt{5577|7247}.

\paragraph{Failure modes at the smaller models.}\label{app:models}
\paragraph{Mistral-7B.} In all three position-local tasks, the benefit is largest at short lengths and decays to zero at the longest length tested (\task{cyclic\_shift}, whose Mistral grid was extended to $N = 256$ with 40 trials per cell: at $N = 256$, $+0.008$, $z = 0.4$; \task{random\_permutation}, $N = 64$: $-0.009$, $z = 0.5$; \task{conditional\_shift}, $N=64$: $-0.006$, $z = 0.4$). In Qwen models the benefit holds or grows with length.

\paragraph{Repetition collapse.} On \task{running\_xor}, Qwen2.5-7B answers with a single repeated character in 30\% of its generations, more often under dense markers. Measured the same way, this falls to 0.8\% at 14B and 0.3\% at Mistral-7B, so it is specific to Qwen2.5-7B.

\paragraph{Out-of-alphabet answers.} On the digit tasks, Qwen2.5-7B often writes a letter (usually \texttt{t}, sometimes \texttt{o}) in place of \texttt{0}, in 22\% to 35\% of outputs, depending on the task (the share whose answer contains a \texttt{t} or an \texttt{o}), with or without markers. On spaced letters, several models step outside the alphabet (for example \texttt{I} after \texttt{H} instead of wrapping to \texttt{A}). Such characters are scored as errors.

\subsection{How the uncertainty was computed}
\label{app:stats}

Pooled effects use the length-matched estimator of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, with analytic
standard errors over generations. Two checks ask how much that choice matters. Both resample 2{,}000 times,
over the 91 pooled task--model cells of the three position-local families and the dependency family at the seven open models. The 125 cells of the main analysis also include the 15 reset cells and the 19 Gemini cells (6 at Gemini 3.5 with 3 trials each, 13 at Gemini 3.6 with 5), which are not resampled. The same 125 split by task group rather than by model are the 62 comparisons on unmodified tasks that Figure~\ref{fig:split} shows, the 48 control cells, and those 15 reset cells.

\paragraph{Resampling generations reproduces the analytic result.} Bootstrapping generations
within each condition and length gives a median $|z|$ within 3\% of the analytic value, and the
Bonferroni verdict ($|z| \ge 3.54$) changes in 1 of 91 cells. The analytic standard errors are
therefore not the weak point.

\paragraph{Resampling whole cells is far wider.} Treating each (length, spacing, alphabet) cell
as the unit instead, so that whole cells enter or leave a resample, widens the median interval
from 0.046 to 0.155 and leaves 16 of 91 cells above the Bonferroni threshold instead of 62. Two
things drive this, and neither is a defect in the data. First, a pooled cell deliberately spans
lengths at which the effect is absent: at $N \le 16$ these models are near ceiling and markers
do nothing, which is itself Finding~2. Averaging those in and then asking how much the average
moves when lengths are resampled mostly measures that by-design heterogeneity. Second, a pooled
cell contains only 19 to 57 (length, spacing, alphabet) cells, depending on the task, and a bootstrap over so few
clusters has little power.

Restricting to the length where the claim is actually made removes the first problem. At
$N = 64$, all 24 spaced-letter and digit cells at the four stronger models have a positive
effect, between $+0.07$ and $+0.37$ (mean $+0.24$); all 24 clear $|z| \ge 3.54$ analytically and
under the generation bootstrap, 21 of 24 have a cell-level $|z| \ge 2$, and 4 of 24 clear 3.54
under the cell-level bootstrap with its 15 or so clusters. Both bootstraps use a fixed seed (20261111).

\paragraph{Stratified standard errors and every control cell.} The analytic standard error of the marker mean, $s/\sqrt{n}$ over all of a cell's marker generations, mixes the systematic differences between lengths, spacings, and alphabets into the sampling variance. Treating those (length, spacing, alphabet) cells as fixed strata instead gives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SE}^2\!\left(\overline{\mathrm{acc}}_{\text{Bounds}}\right) = \sum_c w_c^2\, \frac{s_c^2}{n_c},
\label{eq:sestrat}
\end{equation}
with $w_c$ the share and $n_c$ the generation count of marker generations in stratum $c$, and the same over alphabets within each length for the baseline. The stratified $z$ is larger than the analytic one in 44 of the 48 control cells, since the between-stratum spread no longer counts as noise, and gives the same verdicts at the four stronger models (23 of 24). Table~\ref{tab:controlcells} lists all 48 spaced-letter and digit cells with the length-matched baseline, the marker accuracy, $\Delta$, the share of remaining errors removed, the two $z$ values, and the effect at $N = 64$ alone. A final block gives, per model and format, the three-task means and the model-level $\rho$ of \Eqref{eq:share} that the text quotes; its $z$ columns are dashes because a mean over tasks has no single standard error, as are the cell-bootstrap entries for Gemini, which is not resampled.

\paragraph{The baseline's marker sentence.} The no-marker prompt keeps the sentence that
describes the markers. To measure what that costs, we reran the no-marker condition at the four
stronger models with every mention of markers removed from the prompt, on the same inputs: the ten task variants of the two tokenization
controls and the dependency family, $N \in \{16, 32, 64\}$, every alphabet or modulus value, 20
trials. The paired difference (clean minus with-sentence) is $+0.006$ overall (95\% interval $[0.002, 0.009]$ over 300 cells: 25 task--alphabet configurations by three lengths is 75 cells per model, at four models), between
$-0.015$ and $+0.026$ in every model--family combination, and negative, meaning the sentence
helped, in 105 of the 300 cells. Recomputing Finding~2 at $N = 64$ against the clean baseline
moves the share of remaining errors removed by at most 0.014 at any model. Per model it is $+0.002 \pm 0.006$ at Qwen2.5-14B, $+0.003 \pm 0.006$ at 32B, $+0.007 \pm 0.010$ at Qwen3.8 and $+0.012 \pm 0.007$ at Gemma 4. Rerun at Qwen2.5-7B on the same cells it costs $-0.003 \pm 0.009$ (75 cells), so it does not confuse the smaller model either; over all 375 cells the cost is $+0.0039 \pm 0.0035$.

\paragraph{Why $\rho$ is a ratio of means.} \Eqref{eq:share} divides a mean $\Delta$ by a mean baseline error rather than averaging the task-level ratios, so a task with a large baseline error and a proportionally small $\Delta$ dominates the denominator. At Gemma 4 on spaced letters \task{conditional\_shift} carries 68\% of that denominator and holds $\rho$ to 48\%, against 74\% for the unweighted mean of the three task-level ratios. That weighting is not why dropping \task{conditional\_shift} raises the share removed, though: it has the lowest task-level share of the three in all eight strong-model blocks, so the two-task figure is higher under an unweighted mean of task-level ratios as well. What the ratio of means adds is the size of the drag, which is largest where that task dominates the baseline error. Over the two exact-relabel tasks, \task{cyclic\_shift} and \task{random\_permutation}, \Eqref{eq:share} gives 44\% to 98\% with spaced letters and 55\% to 96\% with digits.

\paragraph{The three estimators on the Finding~2 cells.} On the 24 control cells at the four stronger models, generation-level standard errors make 12 of 12 spaced-letter cells and 11 of 12 digit cells significant, the one exception being \task{conditional\_shift} with digits at Qwen2.5-14B. The fixed-strata estimator agrees in sign on all 24 and makes 23 significant. At $N = 64$ alone all 24 clear $|z| \ge 3.54$ at the generation level, while the cell-level bootstrap, the most conservative of the three, leaves 21 of 24 above $|z| \ge 2$ and 4 above 3.54.

\paragraph{Dropping the redundant configurations.} At $A = 2$ the three position-local rules
coincide and at $M = 2$ \task{running\_sum\_local} computes \task{running\_xor}
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The cross-task aggregate of Figure~\ref{fig:split} therefore leaves them out. Including them instead moves each of its 62 cells by 0.003 in the median and 0.043 at most, changes 28 of the 62 printed two-decimal values, and makes one cell significant that is not (\task{running\_sum\_local} at Gemini 3.6), which is the whole difference between 8 and 9 significantly negative dependency cells. Over the wider family, recomputing all 91 pooled cells without those configurations moves
the effect by 0.013 in the median cell and at most 0.054; the effect gets \emph{larger} in 54 of
91 cells, because the two-symbol cells are the easiest and dilute the average. Three cells change
sign, all with $|\Delta| \le 0.009$ and none significant either way, and the Bonferroni verdict
changes in 5 of 91 cells. Finding~2 is unchanged: 23 of its 24 cells remain significant.

\paragraph{Paired inputs.} The main grids draw a fresh input for every (length, spacing, trial) cell, so the no-marker and marker runs of a trial transform different strings and every effect is an unpaired difference of means. To measure what that costs, we reran the spaced-letter and digit controls and the four dependency tasks at $N \in \{16, 32, 64\}$ with the input of each trial fixed across spacings (20 trials; Table~\ref{tab:paired}). The paired and unpaired estimates of the effect agree to within 0.01 in every model and format, the correlation between a trial's marker and no-marker accuracies is 0.16 to 0.35, and the paired standard error of a cell is 78\% to 91\% of the unpaired one, so pairing would have tightened the intervals by 9\% to 22\%. The paired run's no-marker rows reuse the main grid's seeds and reproduce its outputs exactly in 84\% to 100\% of generations, depending on the model.

\paragraph{Pooled effects on dependency tasks.} The 9 significantly positive dependency cells of Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} are all on \task{running\_xor}, \task{running\_sum\_local}, or \task{sliding\_window\_max} at the stronger open models. Averaged over tasks, the effect ranges from $-0.05$ (Gemini 3.5) and $-0.04$ (Gemini 3.6 and Qwen2.5-7B) to $+0.04$ (Qwen2.5-32B). \task{copy\_previous}, which needs no computation, is negative even where the model is near ceiling without markers (Qwen3.8: $-0.26$). The pattern with length is consistent with this mixture: short sequences admit only dense spacings ($k < N$), so a larger share of their positions sit right after a marker, while long sequences add sparse spacings and are where the within-segment gain is large. At Qwen2.5-32B, \task{running\_xor} goes from $-0.11$ at $N = 4$ to $+0.13$ at $N = 64$, and \task{running\_sum\_local} from $-0.05$ to $+0.14$; Gemma 4's \task{running\_sum\_local} goes from $-0.10$ at $N = 8$ to $+0.19$ at $N = 64$. Qwen3.8 gains most on balance on \task{running\_xor} and \task{running\_sum\_local} ($+0.10$ and $+0.14$): its post-marker positions fail as at every model, but its within-segment positions on those two tasks gain $+0.13$ and $+0.15$.

\paragraph{The alignment, and what it costs.} The alignment is a standard minimum edit distance with unit costs for substitution, insertion and deletion; where several optimal paths exist the traceback prefers the diagonal step, so a position is counted correct only when it is aligned to an identical predicted character, and no bound is placed on how far the alignment may shift. That last freedom has a price worth stating. Scoring two \emph{independent} random strings of length 64 this way gives 0.72 at $A = 2$, 0.52 at $A = 4$ and 0.34 at $A = 8$, against the 0.50, 0.25 and 0.12 that index-by-index scoring gives, so roughly 0.22 to 0.27 of the aligned score at chance is the alignment catching coincidental matches. The inflation is larger where there are more errors to shift around, which is the no-marker run rather than the marker run, so it shrinks the aligned gain and the surviving share of Table~\ref{tab:alignedctl}. Both therefore understate how much of the gain is better per-character computation rather than place-keeping, and the post-marker losses of Table~\ref{tab:aligned}, which survive alignment, survive it in spite of a bias that works against them.

\paragraph{Alignment, cell by cell.} The within-segment gains of Section~\ref{sec:segmentation} shrink but stay positive under alignment: at Qwen2.5-32B the gain drops from $+0.11$ to $+0.03$ on \task{running\_xor} and from $+0.12$ to $+0.05$ on \task{running\_sum\_local}, and at Qwen3.8 from $+0.13$ to $+0.06$ and from $+0.15$ to $+0.10$ (Table~\ref{tab:aligned}); the alignment-sensitive share runs from 13\% to 99\% across the eleven qualifying cells at the four stronger models, with a median of 52\%. Because the share is (index $-$ aligned) / index, it is the part of the gain that comes from baseline phase slips the markers prevented rather than from fewer substitutions.

\paragraph{Error clustering.} Table~\ref{tab:autocorr} asks whether markers change how errors cluster along the sequence. For every generation at $N = 64$ of the four stronger open models, the per-position error indicator is computed under index scoring and under the edit-distance alignment, and three quantities are pooled over generations: the error rate, the probability $p_{1|1}$ that the position after an error is also wrong, and the probability $p_{1|0}$ that the position after a correct one is wrong. Under index scoring a dropped or inserted character makes every later position wrong, so $p_{1|1}$ there measures cascading misalignment; under alignment it measures the clustering of genuine local errors. Without markers the index-scored $p_{1|1}$ is 0.70 to 0.77 on the spaced and digit formats; sparse markers ($k \in \{2,4,8,16\}$) bring it to 0.24 to 0.59, and the mean run of consecutive errors shortens from 3.1 to 3.9 positions to 1.3 to 2.3. Under alignment the change is smaller ($[0.34, 0.51]$ against $[0.21, 0.40]$) and $p_{1|0}$ falls together with the error rate, so sparse markers mostly stop a slip from propagating, with a smaller reduction in the clustering of substitutions. Across the eight control cells the two parameters fall by comparable absolute amounts, $-0.11$ on average for $p_{1|1}$ against $-0.09$ for $p_{1|0}$, with $p_{1|1}$ the larger drop in four of them; in relative terms it is $p_{1|0}$ that falls much further, tracking the error rate down (0.18 to 0.05 at Qwen2.5-32B on spaced letters), so the large cut to the cascade is specific to index scoring, where it is phase slips that cascade. A marker after every character lowers the error rate on the controls but does not shorten the cascades (index-scored $p_{1|1}$ of 0.45 to 0.70). On the dependency tasks the same shift appears (0.63 to 0.79 against 0.40 to 0.61, index-scored), on top of the errors the blind spot adds at the post-marker positions. Read as a two-state Markov error model, $p_{1|1}$ and $p_{1|0}$ are its cascade and fresh-error parameters, and which of them markers lower depends on the scoring. Under index scoring they cut the cascade sharply, because periodic markers stop a phase slip from running to the end of the answer. Under alignment, which repairs those slips, the two parameters fall by comparable absolute amounts, but in relative terms it is the fresh-error parameter that falls much further rather than the cascade: at Qwen2.5-32B on spaced letters, aligned $p_{1|1}$ goes from 0.46 to 0.35, a drop of 0.11, while $p_{1|0}$ goes from 0.18 to 0.05, a drop of 0.13 but a far larger share of its starting value, and at Gemma 4 on digits $p_{1|0}$ goes from 0.06 to 0.01. Error runs are also right-censored at $N = 64$, which truncates the unarrested no-marker cascades more severely than the marked runs, so the fall in index-scored mean run length is a conservative bound. The index-scored effect is therefore about slips propagating, and the aligned effect about substitutions being started.

\paragraph{Symbol, placement, and wording.} On contiguous-letter \task{cyclic\_shift} ($A = 2$, $k = 4$) at Qwen2.5-7B and 32B, where much of the effect is tokenization, the choice of symbol matters less than its familiarity. \paragraph{Larger state spaces.} The dependency tasks above use at most eight symbols. \paragraph{Naturalistic prefix-state tasks.} Two tasks built for this check have natural readings and carry the whole prefix as state: \task{bracket\_depth} (the input is a random string of parentheses; the output at each position is the nesting depth so far, modulo 4) and \task{running\_checksum} (the input is a digit string; the output at each position is the running sum modulo 10). Like the CLRS running minimum, these tasks carry the whole prefix rather than one previous input. Qwen2.5-7B sits near chance on both without markers (0.30 against 0.25, and 0.22 against 0.10), so its cells are not interpretable. \paragraph{Base checkpoint.} The main grids use instruction-tuned models through their chat templates, which a base checkpoint lacks. To compare the two on equal terms, we prompt the base and instruct versions of Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-32B the same way: no chat template, the task's instruction, three solved examples drawn from a seed stream disjoint from the grid (with markers at the same spacing as the query), and the query ($N \in \{16, 32, 64\}$, $k \in \{0, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$, 20 trials per cell; the four dependency tasks and the three digit tasks). Table~\ref{tab:base} summarizes by format. Both effects are present in the pretrained models. At 7B, on the digit tasks the base checkpoint gains $+0.10$ from markers (post-marker $+0.20$, within-segment $+0.06$) and the instruct version $+0.14$ ($+0.30$, $+0.11$); the few-shot prompt puts both 7B models well above their zero-shot grid accuracy on digits (0.55 and 0.48 without markers). On the dependency tasks both 7B models are near chance under this prompt (0.56 and 0.51), the post-marker change is $-0.07$ at the base checkpoint and $-0.03$ at the instruct one, and where the base checkpoint is clearly above chance, on \task{copy\_previous} (0.73), the position after a marker loses 0.20 while the rest of the segment loses 0.03. At 32B both checkpoints are at 0.96 on the digit tasks under the few-shot prompt and gain $+0.04$ each, so this pair says nothing about the long-sequence gain. On the dependency tasks, where both are far above chance (0.77 and 0.76 without markers), the blind spot is nearly as large in the pretrained model as after instruction tuning: the post-marker position loses 0.17 at the base checkpoint and 0.22 at the instruct one while the within-segment positions gain 0.02 and 0.03, and task by task the losses are 0.19 against 0.33 on \task{copy\_previous}, 0.31 against 0.33 on \task{running\_xor}, 0.16 against 0.17 on \task{running\_sum\_local}, and 0.03 against 0.05 on \task{sliding\_window\_max}. Instruction tuning enlarges the blind spot somewhat and does not create it. For the long-sequence gain the base checkpoints settle less: the three-shot prompt they need puts both 32B checkpoints at ceiling on the position-local digit tasks, and at 7B the gain sits at the post-marker position rather than across the segment, so this pair bounds Finding 3 rather than Finding 2.

\paragraph{A finetuned separator.} Can a small finetune teach the model to carry a dependency across a marker? We train a LoRA adapter (rank 16, all attention and MLP projections, two epochs, learning rate $2 \times 10^{-4}$, 16 sequences per step) on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with 2{,}040 examples of \task{running\_xor} and \task{running\_sum\_local} ($M \in \{2, 4, 8\}$) at $N \in \{8, 16, 32, 64\}$ and every spacing $k \in \{0, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$, with the marked answer as the target, so every crossing step is supervised (seeds disjoint from the grid). Two control adapters are trained on the same tasks with the 480 examples at $k = 0$ only, to separate learning the tasks from learning to cross a marker: one for two epochs, and one for nine, which matches the marked adapter's number of example passes (4{,}320 against 4{,}080). All three reach a training loss below $0.001$. The merged models are evaluated with the standard runners (Table~\ref{tab:lora}) on \task{running\_xor} at the grid's own inputs, which the adapter has not seen, on two dependency tasks that were not in training, and on a position-local digit task. On the trained tasks all three adapters are at or near 1.00 without markers (0.98 and 0.99 for the controls on \task{running\_sum\_local}), but the controls' post-marker position falls: to 0.60 on \task{running\_xor} after two epochs ($-0.40$ against their own no-marker run) and to 0.56 after the matched nine ($-0.44$), and to 0.40 and 0.37 on \task{running\_sum\_local} ($-0.57$ and $-0.63$). Learning the task without markers widens the blind spot. The adapter trained on marked targets scores 1.00 at every position of both trained tasks. On \task{copy\_previous} it repairs the post-marker position ($+0.04$, from $-0.11$ in the released model; the matched control reaches $0.00$); on \task{sliding\_window\_max} it does not ($-0.07$, where the matched control reaches $+0.06$). The costs it shows on held-out tasks appear equally in the step-matched control: both lose no-marker accuracy on \task{sliding\_window\_max} (0.60 to 0.43 for both) and both lose the marker gain on \task{random\_permutation} with digits ($+0.08$ in the released model; $-0.02$ and $-0.01$), so those costs come from the amount of finetuning rather than from marker supervision. What marker supervision does on its own is to remove the blind spot on the tasks it was shown, where the same training without markers widens it; on the held-out dependency tasks the step-matched control does as well or better at this size. Set against the worked-example prompt of Section~\ref{sec:blindspot}, which is a two-shot across-boundary demonstration, the finetune repairs the trained tasks completely where the prompt repaired them in part, and the prompt leaves other tasks untouched where the finetune does not. The two-epoch control saw 480 examples against 2{,}040, so the matched control is the fair comparison; the remaining asymmetry is that only the marked adapter ever saw a marker. Qwen2.5-7B is near chance on several of these tasks, so this is a bounded test of the idea.

\paragraph{The separator at Qwen2.5-32B.} The same recipe at Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (one sequence per step with 16 accumulation steps, so the same 16 sequences per update; final training loss below $10^{-4}$ for every adapter) gives a marked adapter, a two-epoch unmarked control, and an unmarked control trained for nine epochs to match the marked adapter's number of example passes. On the trained tasks the result repeats: the marked adapter is perfect at every position, and both controls widen the blind spot well beyond the released checkpoint's. On the held-out dependency tasks the step-matched control separates two cases. On \task{sliding\_window\_max} the repair is specific to marked training: only the marked adapter ends above zero, while both controls sit below the released checkpoint. On \task{copy\_previous} it is not: the step-matched control reaches the position almost as well as the marked adapter does. The cost in accuracy without markers likewise comes mostly from the number of training steps, since the step-matched control pays a similar cost to the marked adapter while the shorter control does not, and on \task{random\_permutation} the marker gain survives in every adapter. \paragraph{A byte-level model.} EvaByte-SFT \citep{zheng2025evabyte}, a 6.5B byte-level chat model, gives one token per character and per marker; its released code needs one fix under current library versions (its rotary position tables are left uninitialized and must be rebuilt after loading). On \task{running\_xor}, \task{copy\_previous}, and two position-local digit tasks it writes the input back instead of applying the rule in 45\% of outputs and stays at or below chance with and without markers. \section{Mechanism details}
\label{app:mechanism}

\paragraph{Head-count dose response.} To test whether the benefit is spread over heads, we repeated the ablation at $N = 64$ and $k = 8$ with the top 50 and top 200 marker-attending heads (2\% and 7.8\% of the model's heads), each with random-head and sham controls of the same size (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}c). Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} gives the shares of the gain removed; the paired drops behind them at 200 heads are $0.155 \pm 0.072$ on spaced letters and $0.115 \pm 0.083$ on digits, against $-0.007$ to $-0.002$ for both controls of that size. Because both controls are null, the drop is specific to removing attention to markers rather than a general disruption. On spaced letters, the drops of about 0.04 for the 10-head conditions and the 50-head controls all come from one trial, the same one in each case; without it they are within 0.01 of zero.

\paragraph{Activation patching.}
\label{app:patching}
To ask where the blind spot is represented, we teacher-force Qwen2.5-32B on \task{running\_xor} and \task{running\_sum\_local} ($M = 4$) at $N = 32$, $k = 4$, 20 inputs per task, twice: with markers (the marked run) and without (the clean run), each prompt followed by its correct answer. At every post-marker answer position we read the next-token distribution and record the log-odds of the correct digit against the reset digit, the input digit at that position, which is the answer a model gives when it treats the segment as a fresh start, and whether the correct digit is the argmax. Qwen's tokenizer gives one token per digit and per bar, so answer positions align between the two runs once the bars are skipped. We cache the clean run's residual stream after each decoder layer and, in the marked run, overwrite the residual stream at one layer, or at every layer at once, at a chosen set of positions with the clean run's values at the corresponding positions: the input digit before each marker, the input digit after it, every input digit, or the answer digit written just before the marker. The log-odds are averaged over the post-marker positions where the correct and reset digits differ, which on both tasks excludes the positions whose previous input is 0, where the two answers coincide: 69 of the 140 post-marker positions remain on \task{running\_xor} (1 to 5 per input) and 100 of 140 on \task{running\_sum\_local} (3 to 7 per input); accuracy counts every post-marker position. Each input contributes the mean over its usable positions, the 20 inputs are averaged, and recovery is computed on those means, as \Eqref{eq:recovery} of Section~\ref{sec:patching} defines it. A ratio is only meaningful when the clean--marked gap is large; it is 7 to 9 nats at Qwen2.5-32B. Table~\ref{tab:patching} gives the recovery at every site and Figure~\ref{fig:mechlayers} the recovery by layer at Qwen2.5-32B; Section~\ref{sec:patching} reads them and draws the conclusion. Three details do not appear there. Without patching, the marked run is at or near chance at the post-marker step (0.46 on \task{running\_xor}, 0.32 on \task{running\_sum\_local}, against 0.79 and 0.47 in the clean run). Patched one layer at a time, the post-marker input digit alone peaks at 33\% and 63\%, at the same depth as the every-input curve. And accuracy recovers far less than the log-odds do (up to 29\%), because the patched log-odds end near zero rather than clearly positive. At Qwen2.5-7B the same procedure is not informative: its teacher-forced clean run is itself at chance at $N = 32$ (0.49 on \task{running\_xor}, 0.28 on \task{running\_sum\_local}), and the clean--marked gap is 0.4 to 1.1 nats rather than 7 to 9. That near-zero denominator makes the recovery ratios swing between $-0.5$ and $1.1$, so Table~\ref{tab:patching} leaves them blank and the figure omits that model. At Gemma 4 the clean--marked gaps are 10 and 13 nats and at Qwen3.8 2.2 and 2.3; Section~\ref{sec:patching} gives their recoveries, which order the sites as at Qwen2.5-32B. Their single-layer curves end differently: Gemma 4's is below 0.05 from layer 44, while Qwen3.8's holds 0.05 to 0.11 as far as layer 58 and vanishes only at layer 59. The best single layer for the input after the marker at Gemma 4 on \task{running\_xor} is layer 0 (0.33): patching that input right after the first layer recovers a third of the gap, recovery then dips, and a second band of 0.25 to 0.29 follows around layers 24 to 27; it is an early-layer value, not an indexing artifact. Where the single-layer recovery from the input digit after the marker falls from one layer to the next, the layer at which it falls is where later positions read that digit, so the two hybrid models show which kind of layer does the reading. At Qwen3.8 it is mostly the softmax-attention layers: its 16 softmax layers account for 62\% of the total decline over the two tasks (0.79 of 1.27 in recovery units), about five times as much per layer as its 48 linear-attention layers, and the largest single drop (0.19, \task{running\_sum\_local}) is at softmax layer 51. At Gemma 4, local and global layers contribute about equally per layer (0.020 and 0.018), as expected, because its 1,024-token local window covers our whole prompts, so the local--global split cannot matter at these lengths. With Qwen3.8's small clean--marked gap, this comparison is indicative only.
