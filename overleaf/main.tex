\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Large language models exhibit a sharp \emph{accuracy cliff} on deterministic, multi-step tasks: performance holds up over short sequences and then collapses well before any obvious capacity limit. A recent line of work models this collapse using a Sherrington--Kirkpatrick (SK) spin-glass framework, in which an \emph{error-accumulation factor} $\alpha > 1$ produces runaway compounding of mistakes. We test a single-pass mitigation for this collapse: inserting periodic structural boundary tokens (\texttt{|}) into the input, with instructions to copy them through unchanged, to interrupt attention-driven error cascades. We evaluate this on three open-weight instruction-tuned models (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Mistral-7B-v0.3) with a full statistical sweep, an expanded boundary-density grid, cross-model validation, and a 20-trial-averaged mechanistic analysis of attention, then extend the headline comparison and the mechanistic analysis to a fourth model, Qwen2.5-32B.

We find the core claim holds, but only under a scope condition the original framing does not state: on the simplest version of the task (a 2-symbol alphabet), boundaries reduce $\alpha$ substantially and consistently in all four models tested on the headline grid (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, and Qwen2.5-32B) --- the cleanest, most reproducible effect in this study. On harder variants (4- and 8-symbol alphabets), the point estimate is inconsistent across models --- present in some, reversed in others --- but a bootstrap analysis shows essentially none of these differences are actually distinguishable from noise, since the underlying accuracy data collapses to floor too quickly to constrain the fit; the honest characterization is inconclusive, not a general property of the intervention in either direction. We confirm the proposed mechanism directly: boundary tokens act as measurable attention sinks ($R^2 \geq 0.98$ across a 20-trial sweep), attention entropy rises under boundaries in 360 of 360 sampled trials, and letter-to-letter attention measurably dips at exactly the token distances boundaries introduce. Beyond this correlational picture, a causal intervention confirms the mechanism is load-bearing, not merely associated with the benefit: zeroing attention to boundary tokens for just the 10 heads (of 784) that attend to them most erases up to 62.6\% of Cellular's accuracy benefit over Base, with the effect growing monotonically with sequence length. We also report a clean negative result, replicated across all four models: on integer addition, a task with genuine right-to-left carry dependency, boundaries make performance \emph{worse}, not better, at every tested length --- evidence that the intervention helps specifically when a task's per-position outputs are independent, and can actively hurt when they are not. Finally, we show that evaluating an instruction-tuned model through its documented chat template, rather than as raw prompt text, materially changes results for at least one condition (an 8-symbol alphabet) --- a general caution for future work evaluating instruction-tuned models on structured tasks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ask a language model to perform a simple, fully deterministic transformation --- shift every character forward in a fixed alphabet, keep a running sum modulo $M$, add two numbers --- and accuracy does not degrade the way one might expect. It does not fade smoothly as the input gets longer. It holds up close to ceiling, then falls off a cliff.

This is not simply the model ``running out of capacity.'' Recent work modeling this phenomenon (Hou et al., arXiv:2511.00763) casts it in the language of a Sherrington--Kirkpatrick spin-glass: the whole-sequence success rate follows

\begin{equation}
\text{SAR}(N) = \exp\left(-\beta_0 \cdot N \cdot \alpha^{N-1}\right)
\end{equation}

where $\beta_0$ is the intrinsic per-token error rate and $\alpha$ is an \textbf{error-accumulation factor}. When $\alpha = 1$, errors are independent across positions and accuracy decays as ordinary exponential decay --- a slope. When $\alpha > 1$, each error raises the probability of the next, and the result is a cliff: a sequence length $N^*$ beyond which success collapses almost completely, computable directly from $\beta_0$ and $\alpha$.

We test a single-pass mitigation: periodically inserting a structural marker token (e.g.\ \texttt{|}) into the input --- with instructions to copy it through unchanged --- to measurably reduce $\alpha$, without requiring the multiple independent model calls that a true divide-and-conquer approach would need. The proposed mechanism: boundary tokens become \textbf{attention sinks}, absorbing attention that would otherwise flow from a token to distant, possibly-already-wrong context, thereby containing errors rather than letting them propagate.

This paper is a systematic empirical study of that idea: we test it across three models spanning two architecture families and two parameter scales, extended to a fourth (Qwen2.5-32B) on the headline comparison and mechanistic analysis; we run a full statistical sweep across an expanded boundary-density grid; we run every attention-mechanism claim as a genuine multi-trial statistical estimate rather than a single illustrative example; and we show that evaluating an instruction-tuned model through its documented chat template, rather than as raw prompt text, materially changes results for at least one tested condition. We report where the effect is robust, where it is not, and where a plausible mechanistic story needs a real caveat once checked against more than one anecdote.

\subsection{Related work}

The attention-sink mechanism this paper investigates is not a new observation in isolation --- it connects to several adjacent literatures. \textbf{Attention sinks} were first characterized as a general phenomenon by Xiao et al. (StreamingLLM, arXiv:2309.17453), who found that LLMs allocate disproportionate attention to a small number of tokens --- canonically the first few tokens of a sequence --- regardless of their semantic content, and that preserving these tokens' KV cache is sufficient to stabilize streaming generation over very long contexts. Follow-up work has shown the same phenomenon extends to \textbf{delimiter and separator tokens} specifically: SepLLM (arXiv:2412.12094) shows that ordinary segment separators accumulate outsized attention mass and can be exploited to compress a segment's information into its separator token for efficient inference, and a broader empirical survey (arXiv:2410.10781) documents when and why sink behavior emerges over training. Our Section~\ref{sec:attention-sinks} finding --- that boundary tokens inserted specifically for this purpose become measurable attention sinks, with mass that grows with density and (more slowly) with sequence length --- is best read as a targeted, causally-tested instance of this general phenomenon, applied deliberately as an error-mitigation intervention rather than observed as an efficiency side effect.

This also connects to \textbf{mechanistic interpretability of attention-head function}. Olsson et al. (arXiv:2209.11895) identify \emph{induction heads} --- head pairs that implement a ``complete the pattern [A][B]...[A]$\to$[B]'' copying operation, whose emergence coincides with a sharp jump in a model's in-context-learning ability. Our causal ablation (Section~\ref{sec:causal-ablation}) identifies a small, specific set of heads as functionally load-bearing for the boundary-token benefit; Section~\ref{sec:induction-heads} tests directly whether these heads overlap with, or are functionally related to, induction heads.

Separately, \textbf{``lost in the middle''} (Liu et al., arXiv:2307.03172) documents that LLM accuracy on long-context retrieval degrades specifically for information placed in the middle of a context, U-shaped around a strong preference for the start and end. That work and this one both concern position-dependent failure in long inputs, but via different mechanisms and mitigations: lost-in-the-middle is about \emph{where} relevant information sits in an otherwise unstructured context, while the boundary-token intervention is about \emph{periodically restructuring} the whole context regardless of where the informative content sits --- the two failure modes are plausibly complementary rather than competing explanations of long-context degradation.

Finally, this work sits alongside --- rather than in competition with --- explicit \textbf{decomposition-based prompting} strategies: least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al., arXiv:2205.10625) and program-of-thoughts (Chen et al., arXiv:2211.12588) both address error accumulation on multi-step tasks by decomposing a problem into sub-steps solved (semi-)independently, closer in spirit to this paper's own true divide-and-conquer baseline (Section~\ref{sec:divide-conquer}) than to the single-pass boundary-token intervention. Our direct empirical comparison in Section~\ref{sec:divide-conquer} --- true divide-and-conquer wins on accuracy at every tested length but costs 3--4$\times$ more compute by $N=64$ --- can be read as a quantified version of the tradeoff these decomposition strategies are implicitly making: more calls, more accuracy, at a real and growing cost that boundary tokens partially and cheaply approximate in a single pass.

Finally, this connects to a broader literature on \textbf{making a boundary carry real state instead of standing in for its absence} --- directly relevant to the tension this paper's own negative results expose (Sections~\ref{sec:error-containment}, \ref{sec:addition}): a marker the model is told ``carries no meaning'' is the wrong tool for a task where nothing in the sequence can safely be ignored. Explicit scratchpads (Nye et al., ``Show Your Work,'' arXiv:2112.00114) have the model write out the running intermediate value at each step rather than a placeholder --- the checkpoint becomes real content, not a divider, which is exactly the design our own state-carrying modsum redesign (Section~\ref{sec:modsum-state-carrying}) tests directly rather than only citing. Separately, training-time digit-order and carry-alignment tricks for arithmetic (e.g.\ Duan et al., arXiv:2310.11984) show that reversing digit order to match carry direction to generation direction helps substantially when a model is \emph{trained} with that convention; our own finding that the same reordering does not rescue a pretrained instruct model applied only at \emph{prompting} time (Section~\ref{sec:addition}) is a real and, we think, underappreciated distinction --- architectural or training-time fixes and prompting-time fixes for the same underlying problem do not necessarily transfer to each other. Three further architectural approaches solve the state-carrying problem at the model level rather than the prompt level: Transformer-XL (Dai et al., arXiv:1901.02860) caches and reuses the previous segment's hidden states via segment-level recurrence, letting state propagate across a boundary without any explicit memory token; Recurrent Memory Transformer (Bulatov et al., arXiv:2207.06881) uses learned memory tokens that carry a compressed representation of prior-segment state forward across a segment boundary; and Landmark Attention (Mohtashami \& Jaggi, arXiv:2305.16300) uses a token per block that the model learns to use for retrieving the right block, which helps retrieval specifically but does not summarize running state the way a checkpoint does. Our state-carrying boundary (Section~\ref{sec:modsum-state-carrying}) is a prompting-time, no-training-required analogue of this same idea --- inject the true state directly as content at the boundary --- and its genuinely mixed result (helps on the harder modulus, hurts on the easier one) suggests the benefit of a real checkpoint scales with how much state there actually is to lose track of, a more specific claim than these architectural approaches are typically evaluated against.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Task design}

The primary task is \textbf{cyclic character shift}: given an alphabet of size $A \in \{2, 4, 8\}$, a random string of length $N$ is sampled uniformly, and the model must replace every character with the next one in the alphabet (wrapping around). In the \textbf{Base} condition the model receives the raw string. In the \textbf{Cellular} condition, a \texttt{|} marker is inserted every $k$ characters (boundary interval $k$); the model is instructed that \texttt{|} carries no meaning and must be copied through unchanged. Boundary density is reported as $1/k$ (0 for Base). The name ``Cellular'' is an allusion to cellular automata: periodic boundaries divide the sequence into fixed-size, only-locally-interacting cells, echoing how a cellular automaton's update rule depends only on a cell's local neighborhood rather than the full global state --- the same locality assumption this paper repeatedly finds boundaries do and don't get to exploit, depending on whether a task's true dependency structure is actually local (Sections~\ref{sec:error-containment}, \ref{sec:addition}).

Two secondary tasks probe whether the mechanism depends on a task's information structure: \textbf{running-sum-mod-$M$}, where each output digit genuinely depends on the cumulative sum of every prior digit (true global state), and \textbf{integer addition}, where correctness depends on carries propagating right-to-left --- the opposite direction from left-to-right generation.

Two accuracy metrics are used throughout: \textbf{TAR} (Total token Accuracy Rate, i.e.\ per-character accuracy) and \textbf{SAR} (Sequence Accuracy Rate, i.e.\ whole-sequence exact match --- an unforgiving all-or-nothing metric that collapses to floor much faster than TAR as $N$ grows).

\subsection{Models}

Three instruction-tuned models were evaluated on the full experimental battery: \textbf{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}, \textbf{Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct} (same family, larger scale), and \textbf{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3} (different architecture family, matched parameter scale to the 7B Qwen model). This design separates ``does the effect survive scaling'' from ``does the effect survive a change of architecture.'' Two further models were evaluated on the headline grid alone as additional scope checks: Phi-3.5-mini-instruct (a 4th model family, Section~\ref{sec:fourth-model}) and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (a scale-up within the Qwen family, which additionally received the same mechanistic deep-dive as the original three models --- Section~\ref{sec:cross-model-replication}).

\subsection{Prompt formatting: chat templates matter}

Every model here is instruction-tuned, and the Hugging Face / model-card documentation for each specifies a required chat template (special tokens wrapping the user turn) for correct behavior. Evaluating these models with raw, unwrapped prompt text instead departs from a documented requirement, not a stylistic choice, so we compare both prompting methods directly to quantify the effect.

We ran the full grid both ways --- with \texttt{tokenizer.apply\_chat\_template(..., add\_generation\_prompt=True)} applied, and with raw prompt text --- and compared. For at least one condition (8-symbol alphabet, mid-range sequence lengths) the two differ substantially: raw-text prompting produces noisy, inconsistent behavior for that alphabet specifically, which resolves into a much smoother, more interpretable trend once the model is prompted in its documented format (Figure~\ref{fig:fig8}). We flag this as a general caution: evaluating an instruction-tuned model outside its trained prompt format does not add noise uniformly across conditions --- it can distort some conditions much more than others, which is exactly the kind of confound that could be mistaken for a genuine experimental effect. All results reported elsewhere in this paper use the chat-templated pipeline.

All results reported below use the chat-templated pipeline unless explicitly noted otherwise (Figure~\ref{fig:fig8} shows both prompting methods side by side, by design, to make the size of the difference visible).

\subsection{Statistical methodology}
\label{sec:stats-methods}

Every accuracy condition (model $\times$ alphabet $\times$ interval $\times$ length) is run for 20 independent trials with fresh random sequences, and every reported error bar is the \textbf{standard error of the mean} (std$/\sqrt{20}$), not raw standard deviation, which for a near-binary metric like TAR/SAR would overstate uncertainty in the plotted mean by roughly 4--5$\times$.

The SK model ($\beta_0$, $\alpha$) is fit via bounded nonlinear least squares (\texttt{scipy.optimize.curve\_fit}, $\alpha$ constrained to $\geq 1 + 10^{-6}$) to each condition's SAR values across every tested sequence length. A fit is only reported when the underlying SAR values are non-degenerate (at least two distinct values, not all exactly zero) --- a condition where SAR is pinned at zero across every tested length carries no information about how fast it got there, and reporting a fitted $\alpha$ for it would be reporting noise as a measurement. Fits that converge exactly to the lower optimizer bound ($\alpha \approx 1.000\ldots$) are flagged separately, since that reflects the fit hitting the edge of what it was permitted to return rather than a well-constrained interior estimate.

\textbf{Scoring conventions and decoding.} For tasks where boundary tokens appear in the model's expected output (cyclic shift, running-sum-mod-$M$), both the prediction and the ground truth have \texttt{|} characters stripped before TAR/SAR are computed --- boundaries are structural, not part of the answer, and are scored as such on both sides of the comparison. Integer addition and Pauli string multiplication never require boundaries in the output at all (the output is pure digits or pure operator/phase characters), so this stripping is moot for those two. Decoding was greedy throughout: \texttt{do\_sample=False} for all three local models --- no sampling variance is folded into any reported accuracy number.

\subsection{Mechanistic (attention) analysis}
\label{sec:mechanistic-methods}

Attention-mechanism claims are evaluated two ways. A full statistical sweep (20 trials $\times$ 6 sequence lengths $\times$ up to 3 boundary densities, one specific model, extracted with \texttt{attn\_implementation="eager"} to make raw attention weights available) establishes population-level statistics for entropy, boundary-token attention mass, and distance-decay. In addition, one small illustrative configuration (alphabet=2, N=16, k=2) is repeated for 20 independent trials with full per-layer, per-head tensor capture, to produce genuine multi-trial-averaged versions of the example attention-matrix figures, rather than relying on a single illustrative example, which can occasionally show an atypical result for a given metric (Section~\ref{sec:entropy-catch}).

All reported attention matrices are cropped to the \texttt{Input: ... Output:} span of the sequence (excluding system-message and chat-template tokens); because softmax attention sums to 1 over the \emph{full} sequence and not over this crop alone, row sums within the cropped view legitimately range from near 0 (early tokens, whose attention mostly lands on the invisible earlier context) to roughly 0.9 (later tokens). This is not a bug in the visualization; it is a direct consequence of the crop.

\textbf{Causal ablation.} The analyses above are correlational: boundary attention mass tracks density, and density tracks lower $\alpha$, but nothing so far shows the attention-sink mechanism is \emph{necessary} for the accuracy benefit rather than an epiphenomenon. To test this directly (Qwen2.5-7B, alphabet=2, $k=2$, matched seeds against the existing headline grid), we first ran a fresh 5-trial diagnostic pass with \texttt{output\_attentions=True} to identify the 10 (of 784) attention heads with the highest mean attention mass on boundary-token positions. We then monkey-patched the model's eager-attention forward pass so that, for exactly those 10 heads, attention weight to boundary-token key positions is zeroed post-softmax and the row renormalized to still sum to 1 --- a clean ``this head can no longer see the boundary'' intervention that leaves every other head, and attention to every other position, untouched. Only the intervention condition was run fresh; Base and normal (non-ablated) Cellular\_Bounds at the identical seeds already exist in the main grid, so this is a direct paired addition rather than a full rerun (Section~\ref{sec:causal-ablation}).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Does the effect hold --- and where?}
\label{sec:results-headline}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig1.png}
  \caption{Every tested (model, alphabet size) combination, sorted by effect size. Each bar is Base $\alpha$ $-$ mean Cellular $\alpha$ (averaged across every boundary density where the SK fit converged); green means boundaries lowered $\alpha$, red means they raised it.}
  \label{fig:fig1}
\end{figure}

Seven of twelve (model, alphabet) combinations show boundaries reducing $\alpha$; five show the opposite. The split is not random: \textbf{every alphabet=2 combination is green, in all four models}, though the size of the effect varies a great deal --- from Mistral-7B's substantial reduction (Base $\alpha = 1.781 \rightarrow$ Cellular $1.206$, one of the largest effects observed anywhere in this study) down to Qwen2.5-7B ($1.212 \rightarrow 1.064$), Qwen2.5-14B ($1.258 \rightarrow 1.111$), and Qwen2.5-32B's more modest one ($1.162 \rightarrow 1.079$). The five combinations where boundaries \emph{increase} $\alpha$ --- Qwen2.5-14B and Qwen2.5-32B at alphabet=8, Mistral-7B at both alphabet=4 and alphabet=8, and Qwen2.5-32B at alphabet=4 --- are all harder variants of the task.

The honest version of the claim is therefore scoped, not universal: \textbf{structural boundaries substantially and consistently reduce error-accumulation on the simplest version of this task, across model scale and architecture family. On harder variants, the point estimate reverses in 5 of 8 (model, harder-alphabet) combinations across all four models} --- but by the time Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap-ci}'s bootstrap analysis is applied, this ``reverses in most cases'' framing turns out to overstate what the data actually shows. Extending the identical bootstrap procedure to alphabet=4 and alphabet=8 for all four models (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap-ci}) finds that only 2 of 27 individual-density comparisons have a Cellular\_Bounds 95\% CI that doesn't overlap Base's own CI at all --- and both of those 2 exceptions favor boundaries, not against them. Essentially none of the apparent reversals, including every density inside the 5 combinations above, are statistically distinguishable from Base once given proper uncertainty quantification. The failure mode is the same one diagnosed for Phi-3.5-mini in Section~\ref{sec:fourth-model} --- SAR collapses to floor by $N=16$--$32$ in these harder-alphabet conditions (for both Base and every Cellular density, on every model), leaving only 2--3 informative points to fit a curve that used 7 points at alphabet=2. This doesn't mean there is definitely no effect in these harder cases --- it means the data collected here cannot distinguish a real reversal from noise, which is itself a more precise and more useful conclusion than either ``boundaries actively hurt here'' or ``reverses in most cases.''

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1B.png}
  \caption{TAR vs.\ sequence length, alphabet=2 only, one panel per model (now four). Dashed = Base; solid = Cellular (mean across tested densities; band = full range across densities).}
  \label{fig:fig1B}
\end{figure}

Restricting to the one condition established as robust in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}, the raw-accuracy picture agrees: Cellular exceeds Base in 25 of 28 (model $\times$ length) combinations. Qwen2.5-14B and Qwen2.5-32B both show the strongest, most consistent separation, holding at every one of the 7 tested lengths out to $N=256$ (14B: 0.532 vs.\ 0.454; 32B: 0.609 vs.\ 0.515) --- 32B in particular shows no sign of narrowing at the longest lengths tested, the cleanest headline result of any model in this study. Mistral-7B shows a narrower but real gap from $N=8$ through $N=256$. Qwen2.5-7B tracks the same pattern through $N=64$, then the two curves converge and cross slightly negative at $N=128$ and $N=256$ --- the one soft spot in an otherwise consistent four-model result.

\subsection{The underlying scaling behavior}
\label{sec:scaling-behavior}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2.png}
  \caption{TAR (top) and SAR (bottom) vs.\ boundary density, faceted by sequence length, Qwen2.5-7B, alphabet $\in \{2, 4, 8\}$. Error bars are SEM across 20 trials.}
  \label{fig:fig2}
\end{figure}

At every tested length, TAR climbs as boundary density increases, and the size of that improvement is largest exactly where it matters most --- at $N \geq 64$, where the Base condition has already sunk toward chance. SAR tells a starker story: it is already pinned near zero for every condition by $N = 32$, and \emph{exactly} zero for every alphabet at every density from $N = 64$ onward (annotated directly on the affected panels, since all three alphabets' markers coincide at the same point and would otherwise look like missing data rather than a genuine unanimous collapse). The practical reading: getting a long sequence \emph{completely} right in one shot is out of reach regardless of boundaries well before the softer, partial-credit metric gives up --- boundaries buy back partial accuracy long after the all-or-nothing metric already has none left to give.

\subsection{Do boundary tokens become attention sinks?}
\label{sec:attention-sinks}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3.png}
  \caption{Mean attention mass landing on boundary tokens vs.\ density, faceted by length, full 20-trial statistical sweep (Qwen2.5-7B).}
  \label{fig:fig3}
\end{figure}

This is the most statistically decisive result in the study: attention mass on boundary tokens rises with density at \emph{every} tested length, with $R^2 \geq 0.98$ in every panel. It is not, however, a length-independent constant --- at the densest tested setting, boundary attention mass climbs from 0.058 at $N=4$ to 0.177 at $N=256$, essentially tripling. What does level off is the \emph{rate} of that growth: the increase from $N=4$ to $N=64$ (0.058 $\rightarrow$ 0.160) dwarfs the increase from $N=64$ to $N=256$ (0.160 $\rightarrow$ 0.177). Boundary tokens become a steadily more important attention target as sequences lengthen, with that importance still rising, just much more slowly, out to the longest lengths tested.

\subsection{Error containment: does the model recover faster after a boundary?}
\label{sec:error-containment}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4.png}
  \caption{P(next character correct $\mid$ previous character wrong), split by whether a boundary token was just crossed. Left: cyclic shift. Right: running-sum-mod-$M$.}
  \label{fig:fig4}
\end{figure}

On cyclic shift, recovery immediately after a boundary is measurably and consistently higher than recovery elsewhere, from $N=8$ through $N=256$ --- direct behavioral evidence that boundaries help contain a mistake rather than let it propagate. On running-sum-mod-$M$, no such separation appears at any length; the two curves track each other closely throughout. The most likely explanation is structural: running-sum's correct output at any position depends on the literal cumulative sum of everything before it, so there is no purely local error for a boundary to contain --- a wrong digit corrupts the running state itself, and a marker cannot undo that. This distinction --- does boundary-adjacency help recovery --- turns out to predict which tasks benefit from the intervention at all (Section~\ref{sec:addition}).

\subsection{What about the boundary actually matters?}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5.png}
  \caption{Three ablations, alphabet=2, all against the same Base reference (TAR at $N=8/16/32/64$: 0.725 / 0.584 / 0.538 / 0.522).}
  \label{fig:fig5}
\end{figure}

\emph{Marker identity} (left panel; \texttt{|} vs.\ \texttt{;} vs.\ \texttt{\#}, fixed periodic interval): all three markers clear Base by a wide margin at every length and show no consistent ranking among themselves (each is briefly ``best'' at some length) --- the symbol's identity or meaning does not matter, provided it is used consistently.

\emph{Placement regularity} (middle panel; periodic vs.\ randomly-scattered, matched total marker count): the sharpest result in this ablation set. Periodic placement stays clearly above Base at every length from $N=16$ on (0.759 vs.\ 0.584 at $N=16$, a 17.5-point gap). Randomly-scattered markers with the \emph{identical total count} fall \emph{below} Base at $N=16$ and $N=32$ (0.528 and 0.491, vs.\ 0.584 and 0.538) and only catch back up to roughly even by $N=64$. Regularity of placement, not mere presence of markers, is doing the work.

\emph{Density vs.\ raw count} (right panel; constant interval, so marker count scales with $N$, vs.\ a fixed count of 4 markers regardless of $N$): a fixed count of 4 markers is very dense at $N=8$ (one marker per 2 characters) and correspondingly strong there (TAR 0.850, beating even Base's 0.725) --- but by $N=64$ those same 4 markers are spread so thin (one per 16 characters) that TAR falls to 0.474, \emph{below} Base with no markers at all (0.522). The density-preserving condition, which keeps adding markers as $N$ grows, stays at 0.622 at $N=64$. Density, not a one-time dose of structure, is the operative variable.

\textbf{Does the marker need to be a single unusual character?} The three markers above (\texttt{|}, \texttt{;}, \texttt{\#}) are all single, visually distinctive characters. We additionally tested two multi-character markers (\texttt{||}, \texttt{<|>}) and two naturally-occurring tokens that already appear in ordinary text (newline, comma), fixed interval=4, same seeds as the single-character ablation grid above.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig18.png}
  \caption{TAR vs.\ sequence length for seven marker variants (single-character, multi-character, and natural-token), alphabet=2, interval=4, Qwen2.5-7B, against the same Base reference.}
  \label{fig:fig18}
\end{figure}

Multi-character markers are, if anything, among the strongest performers: \texttt{||} is the single best-performing marker at $N=8$ (TAR 0.838, vs.\ Base's 0.725) and \texttt{<|>} is the best at $N=64$ (0.656, vs.\ Base's 0.522) --- extending the marker from one character to two or three does not degrade the effect. The natural-token markers (newline, comma) tell a different story, and the one genuine exception to this whole ablation set appears here: at $N=8$, \emph{both} natural tokens actually score \emph{below} Base (0.638 vs.\ 0.725) --- the only condition anywhere in Figure~\ref{fig:fig5} or Figure~\ref{fig:fig18} where a boundary marker underperforms having no boundaries at all. From $N=16$ on, both natural tokens do clear Base, but by a visibly narrower margin than any of the five deliberately-chosen markers, and for comma specifically that margin shrinks to nearly nothing by $N=64$ (0.532 vs.\ Base's 0.522, a gap of 0.010, against gaps of 0.06--0.13 for every deliberately-chosen marker at the same length). One scoring subtlety is worth flagging explicitly: because the standard response-cleanup step truncates a model's output at its first newline (to strip trailing ``Explanation'' chatter), using \texttt{"\textbackslash n"} itself as the boundary marker required disabling that truncation specifically for this condition, since otherwise the model's own echoed boundary would truncate its answer before scoring. The overall picture is consistent with marker \emph{distinctiveness} mattering, not merely presence: a marker's identity is fungible among symbols chosen specifically not to occur in the task's normal vocabulary (any of \texttt{|}, \texttt{;}, \texttt{\#}, \texttt{||}, \texttt{<|>} work comparably well), but a token the model already sees constantly in ordinary text is a measurably weaker structural signal, and can even actively hurt at short lengths.

\textbf{A tokenization caveat.} Marker length in characters is not the same as marker length in tokens, and this matters for how the \texttt{<|>} result above should be read. Tokenizing each marker with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct's own tokenizer, in the actual context these experiments use (alphabet=2, $N=16$, interval=4, 3 boundaries inserted): \texttt{|}, \texttt{;}, \texttt{\#}, the newline, and the comma each produce an 11-token encoding of the full input string, and --- because of BPE merging --- so does \texttt{||}, despite being two characters. \texttt{<|>}, however, encodes as \texttt{<}, \texttt{|}, \texttt{>} (three separate tokens), producing a 17-token encoding of the identical input for the identical nominal $N$. This means \texttt{||}'s result is a genuinely token-count-matched test of marker length: two characters, still one token, performing comparably to every single-character marker. \texttt{<|>}'s strong performance is not: at matched character-level interval, it injects three times the token-level structural signal per boundary that every other marker in this comparison does, so its result should be read as evidence that \emph{more boundary tokens per checkpoint can help}, not as a clean test of whether marker length itself is what matters. This sweep does not contain a token-count-matched multi-token marker (e.g.\ a two-token symbol used at the same overall token budget as the single-token markers), which would be needed to cleanly separate these two explanations --- flagged here as a concrete gap for follow-up work rather than retroactively reinterpreted from the existing data.

\subsection{A negative result: integer addition}
\label{sec:addition}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig6.png}
  \caption{TAR/SAR vs.\ density, integer addition, Qwen2.5-7B.}
  \label{fig:fig6}
\end{figure}

The direction of the effect reverses completely. At every one of four tested lengths ($N=8, 16, 32, 64$), Base is the \emph{best}-performing condition, and TAR falls further as boundary density increases. SAR is pinned near floor almost everywhere regardless of density, so this task is already difficult in absolute terms --- but boundaries make the partial-credit picture actively worse, not merely neutral. The likely explanation parallels running-sum-mod-$M$ (Section~\ref{sec:error-containment} discussion above): a digit's correct output in addition depends on carry state propagating right-to-left, opposite to left-to-right generation order, so it carries the same genuine long-range dependency. Slicing the sequence into visually separate cells does not just fail to help here --- it appears to actively interfere with tracking that dependency across the boundary. We separately tested whether reversing digit order (so carry direction matches generation direction) rescues the effect; it did not fully do so, and whether letting the model show intermediate step-by-step reasoning (a ``scratchpad'') changes this picture returned an inconclusive result (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\textbf{SK fit.} We also attempted the SK $\alpha$ fit for addition, using the same procedure as cyclic shift. It is uninformative for essentially the same reason established throughout this paper: SAR degenerates too fast to constrain a curve. Every Cellular\_Bounds density except the sparsest ($k=16$) fails the minimum-two-distinct-SAR-values criterion outright. Base's own fit does converge, but not usefully: it pins against the optimizer's \emph{lower} bound ($\alpha\approx1.000$, $N^*\approx2.1$ million) --- meaning the SK model finds no detectable cliff curvature in addition's Base condition at all across the tested lengths, consistent with addition's Base TAR declining roughly smoothly with length rather than showing the sharp cliff cyclic shift's Base condition shows. The one valid Cellular\_Bounds fit ($k=16$, $\alpha=1.452$) is \emph{worse} than Base's pinned-at-floor estimate, consistent with the raw TAR result above, but on data too thin to treat as more than a single, unremarkable data point. We do not read this as adding information beyond the raw TAR/SAR comparison already reported --- it is another instance of the SAR-degeneracy pattern flagged in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap-ci} for the harder-alphabet cyclic-shift conditions, here appearing in addition's own data.

\textbf{Cross-model replication.} The result above is not a Qwen2.5-7B quirk: we ran the identical addition grid on Qwen2.5-14B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, and Qwen2.5-32B, and the reversal is unanimous. At every one of the 5 tested lengths ($N=4, 8, 16, 32, 64$), on all four models --- 20 (model, length) combinations in total --- Base's TAR exceeds \emph{every single tested Cellular\_Bounds density}, not just the density-averaged comparison: Qwen2.5-7B (Base 0.502--0.812 vs.\ best-density Cellular 0.124--0.566), Qwen2.5-14B (Base 0.688--1.000 vs.\ 0.141--0.875), Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Base 0.462--0.975 vs.\ 0.115--0.672), and Qwen2.5-32B (Base 0.672--1.000 vs.\ 0.116--0.950). This is the most uniformly reproducible finding in this paper after the alphabet=2 cyclic-shift result itself --- the addition negative result is not an artifact of one model's particular quirks, but a consistent consequence of splitting a genuinely right-to-left dependency across a boundary the model is told to disregard, replicated across every architecture and parameter scale tested here. Full grids in \texttt{results/<model>/addition\_grid\_templated\_v2.csv} for all four models.

\subsection{The cost of the cheap approximation: boundaries vs.\ true divide-and-conquer}
\label{sec:divide-conquer}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig17.png}
  \caption{Cyclic shift (alphabet=2), Qwen2.5-7B: TAR (left) and wall-clock time per trial (right, log scale) for Base, Cellular (single-pass boundary tokens), and true divide-and-conquer (the sequence actually split into separate model calls per chunk, merged after).}
  \label{fig:fig17}
\end{figure}

True divide-and-conquer wins on accuracy at every tested length, and its advantage \emph{grows} with $N$ rather than shrinking: TAR of $0.812 \rightarrow 0.841 \rightarrow 0.880 \rightarrow 0.871$ at $N = 8/16/32/64$, essentially flat-to-improving, while Cellular's TAR ($0.744 \rightarrow 0.759 \rightarrow 0.678 \rightarrow 0.599$) degrades over the same range. But this comes at a real and growing compute cost: divide-and-conquer takes $1.5\times$ as long as Base at $N=8$, climbing to $3.0\times$ the cost of Cellular ($3.9\times$ the cost of Base) by $N=64$, since it requires re-processing the prompt from scratch for every chunk rather than in one pass. Structural boundary tokens recover a substantial fraction of divide-and-conquer's accuracy benefit --- clearly beating Base through $N=32$ --- for close to Base's own compute cost. This is the direct empirical version of the efficiency argument motivating the whole approach: boundaries are a cheap, single-pass approximation to a more expensive but more effective multi-call strategy, not a replacement for it.

\textbf{Implementation details.} Chunks are fixed at 4 characters (matching the boundary-ablation grid's interval=4 condition), non-overlapping, generated independently via separate \texttt{model.generate()} calls on the same single A100 GPU used for every other local-model experiment, and concatenated directly with no re-ranking or boundary-stitching correction --- cyclic shift's per-position independence makes stitching trivial. This would not be expected to generalize as-is to tasks with cross-chunk dependency (e.g. addition), which is precisely why boundaries and true divide-and-conquer are compared on cyclic shift only here, not on the negative-result tasks. Wall-clock time is measured per-call via \texttt{time.perf\_counter()} around each \texttt{generate()} invocation and summed across a trial's chunks for divide-and-conquer; no batching is used for any of the three conditions, so all three costs are directly comparable call-for-call on identical hardware.

\subsection{Uncertainty in the $\alpha$ estimates}
\label{sec:bootstrap-ci}

Every $\alpha$ reported so far is a point estimate from a single nonlinear fit, and Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1} additionally averages across boundary densities --- both are worth stress-testing directly rather than taking on faith. We bootstrapped every (model, density) fit at alphabet=2 (resampling the 20 trials at each tested length with replacement, refitting, 500 iterations per condition; full results in \texttt{results/alpha\_bootstrap\_ci.csv}).

Two things fall out cleanly. First, \textbf{Base condition $\alpha$ is genuinely underconstrained}: 95\% CIs span $[1.02, 2.18]$ for Qwen2.5-7B, $[1.05, 2.24]$ for Qwen2.5-14B, $[1.51, 2.12]$ for Mistral-7B, and $[1.03, 1.76]$ for Qwen2.5-32B --- wide intervals for every model, consistent with SAR collapsing to floor by $N\geq 64$ and leaving few informative points to pin down the fit. Second, \textbf{the well-populated Cellular densities that carry the headline claim are considerably better constrained}: Qwen2.5-7B at $k=1$ gives $\alpha=1.037$, 95\% CI$=[1.019, 1.084]$, comfortably below Base's own lower bound; Qwen2.5-14B at $k=2$ gives $\alpha=1.153$, CI$=[1.133, 1.164]$. The reduction in $\alpha$ from Base to Cellular is not just a point-estimate artifact for these specific, well-supported conditions.

The bootstrap also validates a caution already flagged qualitatively (Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}): fits pinned against the optimizer's lower bound ($\alpha\approx 1.000$) turn out to have exactly the unstable behavior that flag was meant to signal --- Qwen2.5-7B at $k=3$ gives a 95\% CI of $[1.000, 1.679]$, and Qwen2.5-14B at $k=3$ and $k=4$ show the same pattern. These should be read as ``the cliff is very flat or absent here,'' never as a precise measurement, exactly as originally stated --- the bootstrap just confirms that caution was warranted rather than overcautious.

\textbf{Sensitivity to which lengths are included in the fit.} The bootstrap resamples trials at fixed lengths; a separate question is how much the fit itself depends on which sequence lengths are included at all. We refit Qwen2.5-7B's Base, $k=1$, and $k=2$ conditions (alphabet=2) over five different length subsets: all 7 tested lengths, dropping the longest ($N=128, 256$), dropping the two longest ($N=64, 128, 256$), a short-only subset ($N\leq32$), and dropping the shortest ($N=4$). The fit is essentially unchanged when the longest lengths are dropped (identical to 4 decimal places for every condition) --- SAR is already deep in its floor region by $N=64$ for all three conditions, so those points add no additional curve-fitting leverage beyond what shorter lengths already provide. The fit is more sensitive in the other direction: restricting to the short-only subset ($N\leq32$) pulls $k=1$'s $\alpha$ from 1.037 to 1.012 and $k=2$'s $\alpha$ all the way to the optimizer's lower bound (1.000, down from 1.022) --- with fewer points and less curvature visible, the fit has less information to distinguish ``$\alpha$ slightly above 1'' from ``$\alpha$ exactly 1.'' Dropping the shortest length ($N=4$) shifts Base's $\alpha$ from 1.212 to 1.274, a smaller but non-trivial change. The practical implication: this paper's fits are robust to \emph{how far out} the length grid extends (extra long lengths in the floor region are not doing any work), but a fit run on a narrower, shorter length grid than the one used throughout this paper would be less trustworthy, particularly for well-behaved (low-$\alpha$) conditions where the effect is genuinely subtle. Full results in \texttt{results/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct/sk\_fit\_sensitivity.csv}.

\textbf{Connecting TAR and $\alpha$.} Every $\alpha$ reported in this paper is fit to SAR, not TAR, and the two metrics can appear to diverge because of exactly the SAR-degeneracy problem this section addresses: TAR degrades smoothly and remains informative out to the longest tested lengths (Figure~\ref{fig:fig1B} still shows a real, measurable TAR gap between Base and Cellular at $N=256$ for every model), while SAR --- an unforgiving all-or-nothing metric --- is already pinned at floor for most conditions by $N\geq32$--$64$ (Section~\ref{sec:scaling-behavior}), long before TAR stops moving. This is why the paper reports both metrics throughout rather than either alone: $\alpha$ (fit to SAR) answers ``how does the probability of a \emph{perfect} run change with length,'' which is the quantity the SK framework is built around, while TAR answers the more forgiving question of \emph{how much of the sequence} is typically still correct, and remains the more informative metric at exactly the lengths where $\alpha$'s fit is least constrained. A condition can have a well-estimated, clearly-reduced $\alpha$ (e.g.\ Qwen2.5-7B $k=1$, CI $[1.019, 1.084]$) and \emph{also} show a shrinking TAR gap at very long lengths (Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}'s note about Qwen2.5-7B's Base/Cellular TAR curves converging by $N=128$) --- these are not contradictory, since $\alpha$ describes the shape of the whole-sequence collapse curve while the TAR gap at any one specific length is a different, more local quantity that the SK model does not directly predict.

\textbf{Extending the bootstrap to the harder alphabets: are the reversals real?} Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}'s ``7 of 12 help, 5 of 12 hurt'' split is a point-estimate comparison. We extended the identical bootstrap procedure (500 resamples per condition) to alphabet=4 and alphabet=8 for all four models, to directly test whether the flagged reversal cases (Qwen2.5-14B/A8, Mistral/A4, Mistral/A8, Qwen2.5-32B/A4, and Qwen2.5-32B/A8) are genuine sign flips or unstable fits on badly-constrained data --- the same failure mode already diagnosed for Phi-3.5-mini in Section~\ref{sec:fourth-model}.

\textbf{First, the SAR-floor check.} For all five reversal cases, SAR collapses to 0 by $N=16$ or $N=32$ --- for \emph{both} Base and every tested Cellular density, not just one condition. Qwen2.5-14B/A8's Base condition, for instance, is at SAR=0.30 at $N=4$, 0.05 at $N=8$, and exactly 0 from $N=16$ onward; Mistral's and Qwen2.5-32B's A=4 and A=8 conditions collapse just as fast or faster. This means the SK fit for these harder-alphabet conditions rests on only 2--3 informative points ($N=4$, sometimes $N=8$) rather than the 7-point curves available at alphabet=2 --- a much thinner basis for any curve-fit conclusion.

\textbf{Second, the distinguishability check.} Across the 27 (model, harder-alphabet, density) combinations we bootstrapped --- 19 from the original three models, 8 more from adding Qwen2.5-32B --- only \textbf{2 of 27} have a Cellular\_Bounds 95\% CI that does not overlap Base's own 95\% CI at all --- the strictest test of ``is this direction actually distinguishable from Base, not just a different point estimate.'' Both of those 2 statistically-supported cases point toward boundaries \emph{helping} (Mistral-7B A=4 $k=1$: $\alpha=1.058$ vs.\ Base's $1.251$, non-overlapping; Qwen2.5-14B A=4 $k=1$: $\alpha=1.086$ vs.\ Base's $1.654$, non-overlapping); none of Qwen2.5-32B's own 8 harder-alphabet densities clear this bar in either direction (0 of 8), the same pattern as the other three models' non-``helping'' densities. \textbf{Zero of the apparent ``hurts'' comparisons across all four models --- including every density inside all five flagged reversal cases --- have a CI that clears this bar.} Qwen2.5-14B/A8 at $k=4$, for example, looks like a clean reversal by point estimate ($\alpha=1.246$ vs.\ Base's $1.056$), but Base's own CI is $[1.000, 1.781]$ --- wide enough to fully contain Cellular's point estimate, so the two are not actually distinguishable given the uncertainty in either fit. Qwen2.5-32B/A4 at $k=1$ shows the same pattern ($\alpha=1.317$ vs.\ Base's $1.059$, but Base's own CI is $[1.000, 1.863]$).

One further wrinkle this analysis surfaces: Mistral-7B A=8 at $k=1$ produces a bootstrap CI of $[1.020, 5.000]$ --- pinned against the optimizer's \emph{upper} bound, the mirror image of the $\alpha\approx1.000$ lower-bound pinning already flagged in this section for well-behaved conditions. This is a new failure mode worth naming explicitly: just as a fit pinned at the lower bound means ``the cliff is very flat or absent here,'' a fit pinned at the upper bound means the data is compatible with a runaway cliff so severe the optimizer's search range can't even bound it from above --- in both cases, the point estimate should not be read as a precise measurement, and this specific condition should be treated as fully uninformative rather than as evidence of an unusually steep reversal.

\textbf{The honest updated conclusion:} the ``reverses in most harder cases'' framing, read at face value, overstates how much these harder-alphabet results actually establish, across all four models now tested. At the level of statistical rigor this paper otherwise holds itself to, none of the individual reversals survive a non-overlapping-CI test --- they are consistent with noise on fits built from 2--3 informative data points, not confirmed sign flips. This is a materially different and more honest claim than ``boundaries actively hurt on harder alphabets in these five cases,'' and it removes some, though not all, of the mystery originally flagged: there may still be a real, task-dependent reason boundaries interact differently with harder alphabets, but this data cannot currently distinguish that from measurement noise. Full results in \texttt{results/alpha\_bootstrap\_ci\_hard\_alphabets.csv}, \texttt{results/alpha\_bootstrap\_ci\_32b\_all\_alphabets.csv}, \texttt{results/alpha\_bootstrap\_distinguishability\_hard\_alphabets.csv}, and \texttt{results/alpha\_bootstrap\_distinguishability\_32b.csv}.

\textbf{Does the attention-sink mechanism still fire in a reversal case, even though accuracy is unclear there?} Independent of whether the harder-alphabet accuracy comparisons are statistically resolvable, we can ask a sharper question: does the correlational mechanism (Sections~\ref{sec:attention-sinks}, \ref{sec:entropy-catch}) still operate the same way in a reversal case? We reran the full attention/entropy sweep (420 conditions) on Qwen2.5-14B at alphabet=8 --- the specific (model, alphabet) pair originally flagged as a reversal. Boundary-attention mass still scales with density with $R^2 \geq 0.978$ at every one of the 6 tested lengths, matching the alphabet=2 headline result ($R^2 \geq 0.97$--$0.98$) almost exactly. Attention entropy is higher under Cellular\_Bounds than Base in 360 of 360 sampled trials --- the same unanimous result already reported at alphabet=2 (Section~\ref{sec:entropy-catch}) and on the other two models (Section~\ref{sec:cross-model-replication}). The mechanism fires identically regardless of whether the harder-alphabet accuracy comparison shows a net benefit, a reversal, or (as the extended bootstrap analysis above suggests is most likely) statistical noise around no clear difference. This is a sharper and more useful finding than ``we don't know why some harder cases reverse'': the attention-sink mechanism is not sufficient on its own to guarantee an accuracy benefit --- it operates the same way whether or not the task's difficulty level lets that mechanism actually translate into better performance, meaning whatever determines the harder-alphabet accuracy outcome, it is not simply ``did the mechanism fire.''

\subsection{Is boundary attention necessary, not just correlated with the benefit?}
\label{sec:causal-ablation}

Every mechanistic result so far (Sections~\ref{sec:attention-sinks}--\ref{sec:distance-decay}) is correlational: boundary attention mass rises with density, and density correlates with lower $\alpha$, but nothing establishes that this attention pattern is actually \emph{load-bearing} for the accuracy benefit rather than a side effect that happens alongside it. We tested this causally (methodology in Section~\ref{sec:mechanistic-methods}): identify the 10 (of 784) attention heads in Qwen2.5-7B with the highest mean attention mass on boundary tokens via a fresh diagnostic pass, then re-run the headline condition (alphabet=2, $k=2$) with attention from exactly those heads to boundary-token positions zeroed post-softmax and renormalized, leaving every other head and every other attended position untouched. The 10 target heads are concentrated in specific layers rather than spread evenly (top mass: layer 15/head 5, layer 1/head 24, layer 3/heads 22, 24, and 25, layer 0/head 6, layer 16/head 21, layer 21/heads 6 and 9, layer 22/head 5), consistent with the scattered, non-uniform layer clustering already seen in the correlational per-head breakdown (Figure~\ref{fig:fig12}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig19.png}
  \caption{Left: TAR vs.\ sequence length for Base, normal Cellular\_Bounds, and Cellular\_Bounds with the top 10 boundary-attending heads ablated. Right: the percentage of Cellular's TAR benefit over Base that ablation erases, by length.}
  \label{fig:fig19}
\end{figure}

Ablating just 1.3\% of the model's attention heads --- and only their attention to boundary-token positions specifically, nothing else --- drags accuracy substantially back toward Base, and the size of that drag grows with sequence length: 2.2\% of Cellular's TAR benefit over Base is erased at $N=8$, climbing to 35.2\% at $N=16$, 33.3\% at $N=32$, and 62.6\% at $N=64$. At the longest tested length, ablating ten heads' access to boundary tokens alone erases nearly two-thirds of the entire benefit boundary tokens provide. This is direct causal evidence, not merely correlational, that the attention-sink mechanism proposed in this line of work is functionally necessary for the accuracy gain, at least for the small subset of heads that carry most of the boundary-attention mass --- and the fact that the effect strengthens with $N$ mirrors the same length-dependence already seen in the purely correlational boundary-attention-mass result (Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}), reinforcing that this is the same mechanism being measured two different ways.

\subsection{A fourth model family: Phi-3.5-mini-instruct}
\label{sec:fourth-model}

The three models above span two architecture families (Qwen, Mistral) at two parameter scales, but all sit in a similar 7--14B range. We additionally tested \textbf{Phi-3.5-mini-instruct} (3.8B parameters, Microsoft's Phi family), a genuinely different and substantially smaller architecture, on the identical grid, seeds, and task logic as the other three models. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9b-it were tried first as the two most standard choices for a fourth family, but both are gated on Hugging Face with no access granted to the project's token at the time of writing; Phi-3.5-mini-instruct is openly accessible and was used to keep this check moving.

Phi-3.5-mini-instruct turns out to be a substantially weaker model on this task overall than any of the other three. Its \textbf{Base} condition's SAR (whole-sequence exact match) already collapses to 0 by $N=16$ (0.20 $\rightarrow$ 0.05 $\rightarrow$ 0.00 at $N=4/8/16$), and Cellular\_Bounds follows the same trajectory from a higher starting point. This means the SK stretched-exponential fit used throughout this paper is not meaningfully applicable here: with SAR already at floor for both conditions well before the fit has enough non-degenerate points to work with, the fitted $\alpha$ values are curve-fit artifacts pinned against the optimizer's boundary, not genuine measurements, and we do not report them for this model. We instead report the raw accuracy curves directly.

  \centering

Below some minimum capability threshold, both conditions crash to floor almost immediately, and neither the accuracy comparison nor the $\alpha$-based framework has enough signal left to distinguish them cleanly.

\subsection{Does the causal-ablation mechanism matter? Pre-softmax masking vs.\ post-softmax zero-and-renormalize}

Section~\ref{sec:causal-ablation}'s causal ablation zeros attention weight to boundary positions \emph{after} softmax and renormalizes the row to still sum to 1. A cleaner-sounding alternative is to mask the raw attention score to $-\infty$ \emph{before} softmax (exactly how an ordinary causal mask works) and let softmax itself do the renormalization. These two operations are algebraically identical --- zeroing a subset of a softmax distribution's mass and renormalizing the remainder reduces to exactly the same expression as computing softmax over only the remaining positions from the start --- so this is a test of whether that equivalence actually holds in practice (bf16 rounding, implementation details) rather than an attempt to find a different result.

\subsection{Do the boundary-attending heads overlap with induction heads?}
\label{sec:induction-heads}

\subsection{Cross-model mechanistic replication: does the mechanism hold beyond Qwen2.5-7B?}
\label{sec:cross-model-replication}

Every mechanistic result up to this point (attention-sink scaling, entropy, causal ablation) was established on a single model. We replicated the full mechanistic battery --- the 420-condition attention-sink/entropy sweep and the causal ablation --- on Qwen2.5-14B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, and Qwen2.5-32B, at the identical configurations (alphabet=2, matched seeds).

\textbf{The correlational findings replicate cleanly on all three additional models.} Boundary-attention mass scales with density ($1/k$) with $R^2 \geq 0.97$ at every one of the 6 tested lengths for Qwen2.5-14B, Mistral-7B, and Qwen2.5-32B alike (matching Qwen2.5-7B's $R^2 \geq 0.98$), and attention entropy is higher under Cellular\_Bounds than Base in 360 of 360 sampled trials for \emph{all three} --- exactly the same unanimous result already reported for Qwen2.5-7B in Section~\ref{sec:entropy-catch}.

\textbf{The causal finding replicates in direction but not in magnitude, and the picture is genuinely mixed.} On Qwen2.5-14B, ablating the top 10 boundary-attending heads drags TAR toward Base with the same growing-with-length pattern seen on 7B, but far more weakly: 0\%, 0\%, 3.8\%, and 15.8\% of Cellular's benefit erased at $N=8/16/32/64$, versus 7B's 2.2\%/35.2\%/33.3\%/62.6\%. Qwen2.5-32B sits between the two: 0\%, 0\%, 11.4\%, and 32.0\% erased at the same four lengths --- the same growing-with-length qualitative pattern, at an intermediate magnitude. This is not necessarily a weaker mechanism on the larger models --- both 14B's and 32B's Cellular\_Bounds conditions are already at or near ceiling (99--100\% TAR) at this density for most lengths, leaving progressively less headroom for any intervention to move it, causal or otherwise, as scale increases. On Mistral-7B, the ablation effect is noisy and inconsistent (a small negative effect at $N=8$, a small \emph{positive} effect at $N=16$, none at $N=32$, and a large apparent positive effect at $N=64$) --- but this reflects a different confound: Mistral's own Cellular\_Bounds benefit at this specific density ($k=2$) is already tiny to begin with (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap-ci}'s own bootstrap results show Mistral's $k=2$ condition is one of its weaker densities, $\alpha=1.345$ vs.\ Base's $1.781$), so the ``percentage of benefit erased'' metric divides by a small, noisy denominator and becomes unstable. A fairer causal test on Mistral would repeat this at a density where Mistral's own correlational benefit is actually strong (e.g.\ $k=1$, $\alpha=1.054$) --- left as a direction for follow-up rather than run here.

Read together, the four-model picture is: the correlational mechanism (attention sinks, rising entropy) is now a universal finding across every model in this study, with no exceptions. The causal confirmation is real on every model where the Cellular condition still has headroom to move (7B, 14B, 32B), growing systematically weaker as models get better at the headline task and closer to ceiling at this density --- consistent with the causal effect being genuine but naturally bounded by how much accuracy is left to erase, not with the mechanism itself weakening at scale.

\subsection{Filling in the locality spectrum: three new tasks}

Every task tested so far sits at one of two extremes: cyclic shift, where each output character depends on exactly one input character (fully local), and running-sum/addition, where correctness depends on the entire preceding sequence (unbounded history). We added three new tasks (Qwen2.5-7B, 20 trials per condition) to fill in the middle of this spectrum, one per locality tier.

\textbf{Pointwise-local, new domain: digit complement.} Each output digit is the 9's complement of the input digit ($d \to 9-d$) --- the same locality profile as cyclic shift, but a different rule and a different alphabet (digits, not letters), to check the headline effect isn't an artifact of the specific cyclic-shift construction. It replicates the core finding, with the same ``density has a sweet spot'' texture already seen in the original marker-ablation work (Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}): $k=2$ beats Base at every tested length (0.850 vs.\ 0.775 at $N=8$, 0.400 vs.\ 0.389 at $N=64$), while $k=1$ helps briefly at $N=8$ (0.875) but falls behind Base by $N=64$ (0.249 vs.\ 0.389), and $k=4$ hurts at short lengths (0.469 vs.\ 0.775 at $N=8$) but helps at long ones (0.686 vs.\ 0.389 at $N=64$). No single density is uniformly best across all lengths, but \emph{some} boundary density beats Base at every length tested --- the same qualitative picture as cyclic shift, just with a different sweet-spot density.

\textbf{Position-dependent-local: Vigenère cipher.} A repeating 3-element key (shift = key[$i \bmod 3$]) makes the shift amount depend on position as well as the character itself --- still fundamentally local (no dependency on \emph{other} positions' values), but no longer a single uniform rule. This is a genuinely harder task for the model overall (Base TAR is only 0.27 at $N=8$, versus cyclic shift's 0.73), but the boundary effect is unambiguous and consistent: every tested density ($k=1, 3, 6$) beats Base at every tested length, with the densest ($k=1$) the clear best throughout (0.375 vs.\ Base's 0.269 at $N=8$; 0.210 vs.\ 0.123 at $N=64$). Notably, $k=3$ (matching the key's own period, so boundaries always land on the same phase of the key) shows \emph{no} special advantage over $k=6$ (twice the key period) --- density alone predicts performance here, not alignment with the underlying periodic rule. This is an informative contrast with the next task.

\textbf{Fixed-window-local: local block reversal.} This is the genuinely new middle ground --- reverse every non-overlapping 4-character block, so each output position now depends on \emph{other} characters, but only the ones in its own window. This is the first task in the paper where a boundary's placement relative to a real dependency window can be tested directly: \textbf{Cellular\_Aligned} places boundaries exactly at window edges (interval=4, never splitting a window), while \textbf{Cellular\_Misaligned} places them at interval=2, planting one in the middle of every window. The result is unambiguous and one of the cleanest in this paper:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
$N$ & Base & Cellular\_Aligned & Cellular\_Misaligned \\
\midrule
8  & 0.225 & 0.469 & 0.156 \\
16 & 0.297 & 0.534 & 0.297 \\
32 & 0.336 & 0.564 & 0.227 \\

\bottomrule

Aligned boundaries roughly \textbf{double} accuracy over Base at every length. Misaligned boundaries never beat Base, and underperform it at three of four lengths. This is direct, clean evidence for exactly the mechanism this paper has argued for throughout: a boundary helps when it sits at a genuine dependency edge, and actively interferes when it's forced into the middle of a dependency it's told to ignore, splitting a reversal operation the model must still carry out correctly across a marker it was instructed not to shift. Read alongside the Vigenère result above, the two new tasks together draw a clean distinction: alignment with a task's periodic \emph{structure} doesn't matter on its own (Vigenère's key period), what matters is alignment with a genuine \emph{dependency window} (block reversal), which cyclic shift, running-sum, and addition have no way to test, since cyclic shift has no windows to split and running-sum/addition have only one all-encompassing window (the whole sequence).

\subsection{Redesigning modsum's boundary to carry real state}
\label{sec:modsum-state-carrying}

Section~\ref{sec:error-containment}'s negative result for running-sum-mod-$M$ uses the same content-free \texttt{|} marker as every other task --- the model is told the marker ``carries no meaning,'' which is actively the wrong instruction for a task whose entire difficulty is that nothing in the sequence can be safely ignored. The literature's consistent answer to this (explicit scratchpads (Nye et al., arXiv:2112.00114), segment-recurrence memory tokens (Dai et al., ``Transformer-XL,'' arXiv:1901.02860), checkpoint reinjection (Bulatov et al., ``Recurrent Memory Transformer,'' arXiv:2207.06881)) is to make the boundary carry the actual state instead of standing in for its absence. We tested this directly: instead of \texttt{|}, each boundary is replaced by the TRUE running-sum value at that exact point, written as \texttt{[v]} (e.g.\ \texttt{[3]}) --- a genuine checkpoint the model can read and use as its new running base, rather than a divider it's told to disregard. Modulus 2 and 4, interval=4, same seeds as the existing content-free-marker grid for direct comparison.

The result is genuinely mixed, not a clean win, and worth reporting honestly rather than forced into a single story. \textbf{On the harder modulus (4), the state-carrying redesign is unambiguously better}: it beats the content-free marker at every tested length (0.194 vs.\ 0.094 at $N=8$, up to 0.238 vs.\ 0.171 at $N=64$) and beats Base at 3 of 4 lengths (all but the shortest). \textbf{On the easier modulus (2), it's the opposite}: the state-carrying version \emph{underperforms} the original content-free marker at 3 of 4 lengths ($N=16, 32, 64$), even though it still generally clears Base. The pattern that emerges is that a real state checkpoint helps more, the harder the underlying state is to maintain without it --- at modulus 4, where the running sum is genuinely more effortful to track across a long sequence, giving the model a correct, restatable anchor measurably helps; at modulus 2, where the model already tracks the (much smaller, binary) running state reasonably well even with a meaningless marker, the extra checkpoint tokens may add generation overhead without providing information the model wasn't already maintaining. This is a more nuanced answer than ``boundaries can help state-carrying tasks if redesigned correctly'' --- it is closer to ``whether a real checkpoint helps depends on how much the checkpoint is actually needed,'' which is itself a testable, useful refinement of the paper's core claim about when structural interventions pay off.

The modulus-2 result is a genuine exception to running-sum's overall negative framing: boundaries beat Base at every length from $N=16$ on (0.400 vs.\ 0.284, 0.442 vs.\ 0.291, 0.485 vs.\ 0.419), only losing at the shortest length ($N=8$: 0.294 vs.\ 0.425). But this reverses at the harder moduli --- boundaries underperform Base at every tested length for both modulus=4 and modulus=8. This modulus-dependence (helps at the simplest binary case, hurts as the state space per digit grows) echoes the same alphabet-dependence pattern seen for cyclic shift (Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}), and is consistent with a broader reading across this paper: structural interventions are most reliable exactly where the per-position state being tracked is simplest, whether that's cyclic shift's alphabet size or running-sum's modulus.

\textbf{SK fit and SAR regression.} We also checked whether either the SK $\alpha$ fit or a SAR-based (not just TAR-based) regression add information beyond the raw accuracy table above. Neither does, and for the same reason already established throughout this paper: SAR degenerates too quickly. The SK fit converges to a valid, non-degenerate result only for modulus=4's Base condition ($\alpha=1.251$, $N^*=5.29$) --- every Cellular\_Bounds condition at every modulus, and Base at modulus=2 and 8, fail the minimum-two-distinct-values criterion (Section~\ref{sec:stats-methods}) because SAR is already pinned at floor across the whole tested length range. The SAR-based regression (as opposed to the TAR-based regression already reported) is similarly uninformative for all but the very shortest length: at modulus=4, the only length with any SAR variance to regress against boundary density at all is $N=4$ (slope$=-0.044$, $R^2=0.538$); every length from $N=8$ onward has zero SAR variance across every tested density, giving an undefined $R^2$. The practical conclusion is the same one already reached for addition below: running-sum's negative result is a fact about raw accuracy (TAR), not something the SK/SAR machinery can add further curve-fit precision to, because SAR simply runs out of room to show curvature this early.

\subsection{Pauli string multiplication: task definition and results}

This section gives Pauli string multiplication a proper task definition and its own results, rather than the brief scoring-convention mention in Section~\ref{sec:stats-methods}.

\textbf{Task.} Two random length-$N$ strings of single-qubit Pauli operators (I, X, Y, Z) are sampled, $a$ and $b$. The model must compute their position-wise product: at each position $i$, multiply $a[i]$ by $b[i]$ according to the fixed Pauli multiplication rules (I$\cdot$P = P for any P; P$\cdot$P = I for P $\in \{$X, Y, Z$\}$; X$\cdot$Y = $i$Z, Y$\cdot$Z = $i$X, Z$\cdot$X = $i$Y, and Y$\cdot$X = $-i$Z, Z$\cdot$Y = $-i$X, X$\cdot$Z = $-i$Y), producing a resulting $N$-operator string. Separately, each pairwise product also carries a phase (one of $+1, +i, -1, -i$); the model must additionally output a single symbol for the \emph{total} accumulated phase across the entire sequence (equivalent to multiplying all $N$ per-position phases together, implemented as summing their exponents mod 4). This combines a position-local component (the operator string, exactly as local as cyclic shift's per-character rule) with one genuinely global scalar (the single final phase symbol, which depends on every position in the sequence) within a single task --- distinct from both cyclic shift (fully local) and running-sum/addition (every output position depends on history).

\textbf{Results.} Qwen2.5-7B, $N \in \{4, 8, 16, 32\}$, 7 boundary densities (matching the cyclic-shift grid), 20 trials. The model did not once produce the fully correct operator string across the entire sweep, at any tested length or density (\texttt{String\_Correct} rate: 0.0) --- even the position-local component of this task was effectively at floor throughout, for both Base and Cellular\_Bounds, making this a harder floor-effect case than running-sum or addition. On TAR (mean across all tested densities vs.\ Base), Cellular\_Bounds underperforms Base at 3 of 4 tested lengths ($N=8$: 0.145 vs.\ 0.211; $N=16$: 0.129 vs.\ 0.165; $N=32$: 0.160 vs.\ 0.185; only $N=4$ favors Cellular, 0.092 vs.\ 0.070). Given the operator component never leaves the floor, we do not read this as informative evidence about whether boundaries help or hurt a genuinely hybrid local/global task --- the task as constructed is simply too hard for this model at any tested length to produce a signal distinguishable from floor. We report it here explicitly as an exploratory, underpowered result (Qwen2.5-7B only) rather than let it continue to read as a settled finding by only appearing in the Discussion, per the Limitations section above.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What is robust.} Boundaries substantially and consistently reduce error-accumulation for the simplest task variant (2-symbol alphabet), across three models spanning two architectures and two parameter scales. This is backed by a real, checkable mechanism: boundary tokens measurably absorb attention ($R^2 \geq 0.97$--$0.98$), that absorption grows with density and --- more slowly --- with sequence length, attention entropy rises under boundaries with no exceptions across 360 sampled trials, and letter-to-letter attention measurably dips at exactly the positions boundaries occupy. Both of these correlational findings now replicate cleanly on all three models in this study, not just one (Section~\ref{sec:cross-model-replication}). Critically, the mechanism is also confirmed causally rather than only correlationally (Section~\ref{sec:causal-ablation}): ablating attention to boundary tokens for just the 10 heads (of 784) that attend to them most erases up to 62.6\% of Cellular's accuracy benefit at the longest tested length on Qwen2.5-7B, with the effect strengthening as sequences get longer; the same direction replicates on Qwen2.5-14B, though far more weakly, since 14B's Cellular condition is already near ceiling at this density and has little headroom left to move (Section~\ref{sec:cross-model-replication}). The effect does not depend on the specific marker character used, extending naturally to multi-character markers as well (Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}), is not an artifact of one instruction phrasing, and the intervention is measurably cheaper than the true divide-and-conquer alternative it approximates (Section~\ref{sec:divide-conquer}): true divide-and-conquer wins on accuracy at every tested length, but at 3--4$\times$ the compute cost by $N=64$, while boundaries recover much of that accuracy benefit for close to Base's own cost.

\textbf{What is not robust.} The effect does not generalize cleanly to harder task variants. Across two harder alphabets in all four models, the point estimate reverses in 5 of 8 cases --- but this overstates what the data actually shows: Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap-ci}'s extended bootstrap analysis finds that essentially none of these reversals (or the point-estimate ``wins'') are statistically distinguishable from Base at all, because SAR collapses to floor by $N=16$--$32$ in every harder-alphabet condition on every model, leaving only 2--3 informative points to constrain a curve that used 7 at alphabet=2. Only 2 of 27 individual-density comparisons clear a non-overlapping-CI bar, and both favor boundaries. The honest characterization is therefore ``inconclusive due to data starvation,'' not ``reverses in most harder cases'' --- there may still be a real, task-dependent reason boundaries behave differently on harder alphabets, but this data cannot currently distinguish that from noise. It also does not transfer to tasks with genuine long-range state --- running-sum-mod-$M$ shows no boundary-adjacent recovery advantage at all, and integer addition shows boundaries actively hurting at every tested length on all four models tested (Section~\ref{sec:addition}), plausibly because carries propagate in the opposite direction from generation. Reversing digit order to align carry direction with generation order did not fully rescue the effect, and allowing the model an explicit reasoning scratchpad before committing to an answer produced an inconclusive result (more than half of trials did not even yield a scoreable output in the expected format). A third, structurally different benchmark task (Pauli string multiplication, chosen to combine a position-local component with a genuinely global one within a single task) was also inconclusive: the model did not once get the position-local component fully correct across the entire sweep, and overall accuracy under boundaries was worse than Base at three of four tested lengths. A fourth model family, Phi-3.5-mini-instruct, adds a further boundary condition rather than a counter-example: this smaller, weaker model's accuracy collapses to floor so quickly in \emph{both} conditions that neither the raw-accuracy comparison nor the SK fit has enough length range left to cleanly resolve the effect one way or the other (Section~\ref{sec:fourth-model}) --- a caution that the framework requires a baseline level of model competence at the task before it can be evaluated at all, distinct from the effect itself failing to hold.

We also directly tested the specific mechanistic hypothesis that boundaries should increase attention \emph{clustering} (the intuitive idea that periodicizing context into semi-independent cells should raise graph-theoretic modularity of the attention pattern). The data show the opposite, with strong statistical support ($R^2 = 0.66$--$0.76$, $p < 10^{-14}$ at every sequence length from $N=16$ to $N=256$): clustering coefficient \emph{decreases} under boundaries as density increases. We report this plainly as a case where a plausible-sounding mechanistic hypothesis, derived from the same intuition that motivates the whole intervention, did not survive contact with the data --- attention sinks and rising entropy are real and well-supported; the clustering account of \emph{why} is not, at least not in the form we tested it.

\textbf{Methodological caution.} The chat-template comparison is, in our view, as important a finding as any accuracy result in this paper. It changed at least one condition (8-symbol alphabet) from noisy and inconsistent to smooth and interpretable, and it is the kind of difference that would not announce itself --- the model still produces plausible-looking text either way. Any future work evaluating instruction-tuned models on structured, deterministic tasks should verify the prompting format matches what the model was actually trained on before drawing conclusions from accuracy differences between conditions.

\section{Limitations}

This section collects scope caveats that are discussed in context throughout the paper but are gathered here for ease of citation, distinct from ``What is not robust'' above (which explains \emph{why} the effect doesn't generalize, not what this study itself didn't cover):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The deepest mechanistic analysis (multi-trial attention-matrix figures, prompt-robustness check, chat-template ablation) was run on Qwen2.5-7B only.} The correlational core of it (attention-sink density scaling, entropy) replicates on Qwen2.5-14B, Mistral-7B, and Qwen2.5-32B (Section~\ref{sec:cross-model-replication}); the single-head-level, multi-trial-averaged figures (10--17) were not rerun on every model.
\item \textbf{The causal ablation (Section~\ref{sec:causal-ablation}) targets one fixed set of 10 heads per model}, identified from a 5-trial diagnostic pass. A larger diagnostic sample, or a systematic sweep over different values of $k$ (number of heads ablated), was not performed.
\item \textbf{Every reported number uses greedy decoding} (\texttt{do\_sample=False}). This removes sampling variance from the comparison but does not characterize how these findings would look under realistic sampled deployment (temperature $>$ 0, nucleus sampling).
\item \textbf{The carry-aligned addition placement is a confounded experiment, not a clean negative result} --- restructuring the input into interleaved digit-column pairs changes the input format enough that the model appears unable to parse the task at all, independent of anything about carry alignment specifically.
\item \textbf{The Mistral-7B causal ablation (Section~\ref{sec:cross-model-replication}) is unreliable at the tested density} ($k=2$), because Mistral's own boundary benefit there is already small, making the ``percentage of benefit erased'' denominator noisy; we did not rerun it at a density where Mistral's own effect is stronger.
\item \textbf{Sample sizes for several of the newer ablations are 20 trials per length}, matching the rest of the paper's convention but still modest for effects near the noise floor (e.g.\ the natural-token marker comparison at $N=8$).
\item \textbf{The addition and running-sum-mod-$M$ tasks were never bootstrapped or SK-fit as thoroughly as the headline cyclic-shift task} --- the SK fits and SAR regression that do exist for these tasks are now reported, but neither received the full 500-iteration bootstrap treatment applied to cyclic shift in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap-ci}.
\item \textbf{Pauli string multiplication was tested on Qwen2.5-7B only}, with a single density grid, and should be read as an exploratory third benchmark rather than a fully-developed task on the same footing as cyclic shift or addition.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Periodic structural boundary tokens measurably reduce error-accumulation and delay the accuracy cliff --- but as a real, mechanistically-grounded, and \emph{scoped} finding, not a universal one. The effect is strong and highly reproducible on the simplest task variant, across model scale and architecture family, and is backed by convergent mechanistic evidence at the attention level: boundary tokens act as attention sinks, entropy rises under boundaries with no exceptions in 360 sampled trials, and letter-to-letter attention measurably interrupts at exactly the positions boundaries occupy. The intervention's behavior on harder task variants (larger alphabets) is best described as inconclusive rather than a confirmed reversal: none of the point-estimate reversals survive statistical scrutiny once properly bootstrapped (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap-ci}), a data-starvation artifact from SAR collapsing to floor rather than evidence the intervention actively hurts there. On tasks with genuine long-range state, the picture is clearer and more negative: integer addition shows boundaries actively hurting at every tested length. The honest, publishable claim is the narrower one: this is a real, cheap, mechanistically-understood intervention for the class of tasks where each output position is otherwise independent of the others, not a general-purpose fix for the accuracy cliff.
